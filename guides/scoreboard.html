stop refreshing the number

hey, it's KP. you commented SCOREBOARD, so here it is. the reel was me admitting the habit. this is the research on what it costs, the three-day audit that gives you your real number, and the schedule i replaced it with.

who this is for

you, if you open a brokerage app, a stripe dashboard, an analytics tab or a bank balance more than twice a day and call it work. skip this if you're actively managing a position that needs watching, or if the number you keep checking is a bill you genuinely can't cover — that's not a habit, that's a situation, and it needs a different guide.

why this matters

watching the number feels like diligence. it's the closest thing to work you can do with one thumb. that's exactly why it's so hard to see — it sits in the same slot in your day that actual work would have gone in, and it leaves the same residue of having done something.

and there's a measured cost on both sides of it. more frequent feedback has been shown to make people more conservative and worse off in controlled experiments. more frequent trading has been shown, in a study of tens of thousands of real accounts, to line up with materially lower returns. checking is upstream of both.

what the feedback frequency actually does to you

this is the finding that changed how i set my day up.

Thaler, Tversky, Kahneman and Schwartz ran an experiment published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 1997 on how often people see results. the headline from their own abstract: "the investors who got the most frequent feedback (and thus the most information) took the least risk and earned the least money."

read that twice, because it's counterintuitive. the people with more information did worse. not because the information was wrong, but because seeing every fluctuation meant seeing every small loss, and each one triggers a response that's out of proportion to what it means over a full horizon. the term for it is myopic loss aversion. in plain terms: the more often you look, the more losses you see, and the smaller you play.

what the real-account data shows

the lab result has a large, messy, real-world cousin.

Barber and Odean studied 66,465 households at a discount brokerage from February 1991 to January 1997. the average household earned a net annual return of 16.4% against a market benchmark of 17.9%. the 20% of households that traded most earned 11.4% against that same 17.9%.

be precise about what that does and doesn't say. it's a study of trading, not of checking, and it's one market period. checking isn't measured there. but checking is the thing that produces the urge to act, and acting is the thing that got measured. i'm not telling you a look costs you money. i'm telling you the behaviour that looks produce has a price tag on it, and someone counted it.

the three-day audit

you do not know your number. everyone guesses low, including me.

i thought i checked four times a day. i logged it and it wasn't close. don't estimate this — estimating is how the habit protects itself. tally it for three days, including one weekend day, and write the trigger next to each one. the trigger column is the part that actually changes behaviour, because after three days you'll see that it's four or five situations doing almost all of it.

THE CHECK LOG — 3 days, one of them a weekend

keep this in a notes app. one line per check. no judgement, just tally.

  TIME  | WHAT I OPENED     | TRIGGER              | DID I ACT?
  ------|-------------------|----------------------|------------
  07:12 | brokerage         | woke up, phone in hand| no
  09:40 | stripe            | finished a hard task  | no
  11:05 | bank balance      | saw someone's post    | no
  ...   |                   |                       |

TOTALS
  checks per day, average:            ______
  of those, how many led to an action: ______
  ACTION RATE = actions / checks:      ______%

THE MATH THAT LANDS
  checks/day x 365 = ______ per year
  checks/day x 40 seconds = ______ minutes/day
  x 365 / 60 = ______ hours a year of watching a number
               you did not move by watching it

THE TOP TRIGGERS
  list the three triggers that produced the most checks. these
  three are your entire problem. not the app. the three moments.

the check schedule

you're not quitting the number. you're putting it on a timetable.

going cold turkey fails within a week, because the number genuinely does need looking at sometimes. the move is to decide in advance when you look, so that the decision is already made when the urge shows up and there's nothing to negotiate. write this out, put the review slots in your calendar as real events, and treat an unscheduled check the same way you'd treat any other broken commitment.

THE CHECK CONTRACT — fill in, print, put it near the desk

WHAT I CHECK           HOW OFTEN I ACTUALLY NEED TO     WHEN
---------------------------------------------------------------
brokerage / portfolio  monthly is plenty                 1st, 6pm
business revenue       weekly for decisions              fri, 4pm
bank balance           weekly + before any spend         fri, 4pm
analytics / followers   weekly, and only the 7-day view  fri, 4pm
[your own]             ______                            ______

RULES
1. one review block a week. thirty minutes. everything gets looked
   at in that block and nowhere else.
2. outside the block, the answer to "should i check" is already no.
   you decided on friday.
3. if a number genuinely needs daily eyes, set an alert with a
   threshold instead. an alert comes to you. a check is you going
   to it, and only one of those two is a habit.
4. the one legitimate exception: a specific decision on the table
   today that the number changes. write the decision down first.
   if you can't name it in one sentence, it isn't one.

the scoreboard you're allowed to watch

the habit isn't really about money. it's about wanting daily evidence that you're not wasting your life.

that want is legitimate. the problem is you attached it to a number you don't control on a daily timescale. outputs move slowly, arrive lumpy, and mostly reflect work you did months ago. inputs move every single day and are entirely yours. so build the daily scoreboard out of inputs and let the output get looked at once a week, where it belongs.

THE INPUT BOARD — daily, 30 seconds, tick or blank

pick 3 to 5 inputs. each one must be:
  - fully in your control (no other human required)
  - doable on your worst day
  - a plausible cause of the number you keep refreshing

  INPUT                          | M T W T F S S | week
  -------------------------------|---------------|------
  1 outreach message sent        | x x   x x     |  4/7
  90 min on the one hard thing   | x   x x       |  3/7
  1 piece of work published      | x     x       |  2/7
  no unscheduled checks today    | x x x   x     |  4/7

FRIDAY REVIEW — in the same block as the numbers
  which input did i hit least, and what was the actual obstacle?
  which input, if i hit it 7/7 next week, would move the output most?
  one input to drop. one to keep. do not add a fifth.

if the input board is green for three weeks and the output hasn't
moved at all, the inputs are wrong. change the inputs. that is a
real finding and it only shows up because you tracked them.

the friction setup

willpower loses to a two-tap habit. change the taps.

none of the above survives a phone that makes checking effortless. this takes about ten minutes once and does more than a month of discipline. the goal isn't to make it impossible — it's to insert enough seconds between the urge and the number that the urge has to survive being noticed, which it usually doesn't.

TEN MINUTES, ONCE

[ ] remove every money and analytics app from the home screen.
    do not delete them. bury them. search-only.
[ ] turn off all balance, deposit and milestone notifications.
    keep only threshold alerts you set deliberately.
[ ] log out of the browser versions. no saved password.
    30 seconds of friction kills most of the impulse checks.
[ ] remove any widget showing a number on the lock screen.
[ ] one physical object on the desk that isn't a screen —
    the input board on paper. that's the thing you look at when
    the hand goes reaching.
[ ] put the friday review block in the calendar now, recurring,
    with the word "numbers" in it.

THE LINE — say it out loud when you catch yourself mid-reach
  "the number didn't move because nobody did anything to move it.
   what's the thing?"
then go do the thing. the check is still available on friday.

good to know

  • the QJE result is a controlled experiment about feedback frequency, and the Barber and Odean result is about trading frequency in one six-year market period. neither one measured "checking your phone". i'm connecting them because checking is the input to both, but that connection is my reasoning, not their finding.
  • myopic loss aversion has had mixed replication results — some later studies failed to reproduce the effect cleanly. it's a well-supported idea, not settled physics. the practical advice survives either way, because the time cost is real regardless of whether the behavioural mechanism holds.
  • the audit is the part everyone skips and it's the only part that reliably works. reading about the habit changes nothing. seeing your own tally with the action rate next to it — mine was under 5%, meaning 95% of the checks produced literally nothing — is what actually made me stop.
  • week two is where this breaks. the urge doesn't fade smoothly, it spikes right when something good or bad happens, which is exactly when the check feels most justified. that's the moment the contract exists for. that's why it's written down in advance.
  • watching the number is the closest thing to work you can do without working, and it will happily eat a year. you don't win by watching it. you win by doing the thing that moves it, and then having a life while it moves. that's Off-Script Rich.

proof

  • Thaler, Tversky, Kahneman & Schwartz (1997), Quarterly Journal of Economics — "the investors who got the most frequent feedback took the least risk and earned the least money"
  • Barber & Odean (2000), Journal of Finance — 66,465 households, Feb 1991 to Jan 1997: average net return 16.4% vs 17.9% market, most active fifth 11.4%
  • Judgment and Decision Making — "Myopic loss aversion: potential causes of replication failures", the honest limits on the effect
