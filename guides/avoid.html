the work is not the problem

hey, it's KP. you commented AVOID, so here it is. the reel gave you the line. this is the decoder, the ten-minute test you run on yourself for a week, and the exact words for the three things you've been sitting on.

who this is for

for you if there's one specific thing you've moved to tomorrow more than three times and you can picture it right now. skip it if you're avoiding everything, in every part of your life, and have been for months — that isn't a planning problem and a page on the internet is the wrong tool for it.

why this matters

here's the part i couldn't fit in forty seconds. researchers put people who were highly anxious about math into a scanner. when those people were told math was coming, regions tied to visceral threat detection and the experience of pain lit up. while they were actually doing the math, that relationship didn't show up. the authors' own line: it's not that math itself hurts — the anticipation of math is painful. swap math for the video you haven't posted.

and the reason you keep choosing the dread over the doing: the procrastination literature frames it as short-term mood repair. the phrase researchers use is "we give in to feel good". the present you gets relief in exchange for handing the bill to the you that exists on thursday. you're not lazy and you're not disorganized. you are regulating a feeling, badly, on a timescale of about four minutes.

what you're actually running from

you can't solve a problem you've named wrong, and almost everybody names this one wrong.

"i need to stop procrastinating" is a useless sentence because it treats avoidance as a single thing with a single fix, usually discipline. it isn't. underneath it there is always one specific feeling, and each feeling has a completely different counter-move. the fix for boredom makes doubt worse. the fix for doubt is a disaster if what you actually have is confusion. so you name the feeling first, then you pick the move. takes ninety seconds and it's the only reason any of the rest of this works.

the avoidance decoder

one page that turns the feeling into the next physical action.

print it or copy it into your notes. the honest answer is usually the second one you think of — the first is the socially acceptable version. and pay attention to the confusion row, because that one isn't avoidance at all and forcing yourself through it just produces a worse version of the thing.

AVOIDANCE DECODER

the task: _______________________________________
times moved: ____

which feeling is actually there?

BOREDOM     the task is defined and dull.
            → 25 minutes, no input, do it badly on purpose.

DOUBT       you don't know if it's good enough.
            → ship the version you have. ask one person
              after it's out, not before.

CONFUSION   step one isn't defined. this is NOT avoidance.
            → stop. write the next physical action in
              eight words or fewer. that's the whole task.

JUDGMENT    you're scared of what someone will think.
            → name the someone, out loud. would you trade
              your year for their opinion? then post it.

RESENTMENT  you don't want to do this at all.
            → the real answer might be no. read
              "good to know" before you force it.

OVERWHELM   it's too big to hold in your head.
            → cut it until one piece fits in 20 minutes.
              do that piece. re-cut what's left.

the move i'm taking: ____________________________
when: __________________ (a clock time, not "later")

the ten-minute proof

stop taking my word for it. get five days of your own data.

the claim is that doing the work is less stressful than avoiding it. you don't believe it, because your forecast tells you otherwise every single morning, and you've never once checked the forecast against what happened. so check it. the rule that makes this work is that you are genuinely allowed to stop at ten — if it's a trick to get you to forty, your brain will spot it by day two and the dread number will stop being honest.

THE TEN-MINUTE PROOF — five days, then read it

before you start:
    task                        _____________________
    dread right now, 0-10       ____

set a timer for 10 minutes. you may genuinely stop at 10.

at the ten-minute mark:
    how bad is it ACTUALLY, 0-10    ____
    do you want to keep going? y/n  ____

log:
    day 1   dread ___   actual ___   kept going ___
    day 2   dread ___   actual ___   kept going ___
    day 3   dread ___   actual ___   kept going ___
    day 4   dread ___   actual ___   kept going ___
    day 5   dread ___   actual ___   kept going ___

reading it:
  actual LOWER than dread most days
      → your forecast is broken. you now have proof.
        stop negotiating with it in the morning.
  actual HIGHER than dread most days
      → the task is genuinely wrong for you, or genuinely
        too big. that is real information, not a failure.
        go back to the decoder and check RESENTMENT
        and OVERWHELM.

if-then, not to-do

the planning format that has actual evidence under it, instead of vibes.

a to-do list asks you to decide again, at the exact moment your resistance is highest. an if-then plan makes the decision once, in advance, and attaches it to a cue so the moment itself pulls the trigger. the meta-analysis on this covered 94 independent tests and found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = .65. that is a big number for something that takes eleven seconds to write. the whole thing lives or dies on the cue being specific.

IF-THEN PLANS

format:  IF [situation, time or place], THEN I WILL [action]

bad cue:   "when i have time"  /  "this week"
           "when i feel ready"  /  "tomorrow morning"
good cue:  "when i close my laptop at work"
           "when the kettle goes on saturday"
           "when i sit down after dinner"

fill these in:

posting   IF i finish my coffee on sunday, THEN i will
          record one take and post it without rewatching.

the call  IF it turns 10:05 on tuesday, THEN i will call
          ____________ before i open anything else.

the email IF i open my laptop tomorrow, THEN the first
          thing on the screen is the reply to ____________ .

the ask   IF ____________ replies to my next message,
          THEN i will say the number in the same thread.

the ship  IF it is friday 4pm, THEN whatever exists goes
          out in the state it's in.

and one for the wobble:
          IF i notice myself opening a new tab, THEN i
          will close it and start a 10-minute timer.

the three messages you haven't sent

most of what people avoid is a single message, and most of the delay is not having the words.

so here are the words. these are built to be short, to open with the uncomfortable part instead of burying it, and to not ask the other person to manage your feelings about being late. the send rule matters as much as the text: one read-through, then send. three read-throughs is how a message becomes a draft folder.

1 — THE OVERDUE REPLY (you went quiet, it's been weeks)

"hey — this is late and that's on me. i sat on it because
[the honest reason, one clause, no essay]. no excuse.
here's where i've landed: [the actual answer]. still up
for [the thing] if you are."

2 — THE ASK (money, rate, a favor)

"before we go further i want to be straight about the
number. for [scope], it's [amount]. that covers [what's
included]. if that's outside what you've got, say so and
i'll tell you honestly whether i can make it work —
no hard feelings either way."

3 — THE THING YOU'VE BEEN NOT SAYING TO SOMEONE CLOSE

"i want to say something i've been putting off, and i've
been weird lately because of it, not because of you.
[the thing. one sentence. no preamble.] i'm not asking
you to fix it. i just didn't want to keep carrying it
around you."

send rule: one read-through. not three. send it.

the version of you who does this today and the version who does it in three weeks perform identical work. one of them just paid three weeks of interest on it in dread. stop paying interest on things you were always going to do. that's Off-Script Rich.

good to know

  • if-then only works if the cue actually happens. "when i have time" is not a cue, it's a wish with a calendar next to it. the cue has to be a moment you'd notice even if you weren't looking for it — a door, a kettle, a clock time, closing a laptop.
  • some avoidance is correct information. a task you've moved eight times and still hate at minute ten might be something you should decline, delegate or delete. the decoder has a resentment row for a reason. run it honestly before you grind through — forcing yourself to finish work you never should have accepted is not discipline, it's just an expensive way to learn to say no.
  • the ten-minute proof fails on undefined tasks. if you can't write the next physical action in eight words, you don't have an avoidance problem. you have a missing decision, and no timer, app or amount of willpower has ever fixed a missing decision.
  • this page has a limit. if the avoidance covers everything, has run for months, and comes with not sleeping or not eating properly, that's outside what a checklist can do. talk to an actual person about it. it isn't a character flaw and it doesn't get solved by trying harder on tuesday.

proof

  • Lyons & Beilock, PLOS ONE (2012) — pain-network activation appeared in anticipation of math, not during it
  • Sirois & Pychyl, Social and Personality Psychology Compass (2013) — procrastination as short-term mood repair, "we give in to feel good"
  • Gollwitzer & Sheeran, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology (2006) — implementation intentions, 94 independent tests, d = .65
