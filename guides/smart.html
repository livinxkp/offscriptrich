life SMART

smart people usually do know

hey, it's KP. you commented SMART, so here it is. the reel was the argument. this is the version with the actual numbers behind it, the one place where i was partly wrong, and a test that tells you how accurate your read on yourself really is.

who this is for

you, if you've spent years downplaying what you're good at because saying it out loud felt arrogant, and you've noticed the people around you doing no such thing. skip this if what you actually want is permission to be insufferable — the last section of this guide is specifically designed to take that away from you.

why this matters

an inaccurate self-read costs you real things. underrate yourself and you don't apply, don't quote your number, don't say the idea in the room. overrate yourself and you skip the reps you needed and find out three years late.

so the interesting question isn't "is it arrogant to think you're smart." it's how accurate are you, and the answer is measurable. there's a meta-analysis on it, and there's a test in this guide that gives you your own number in about four weeks.

the number nobody quotes

your self-estimate is real information. it's just noisy information.

Freund and Kasten published a meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin in 2012, pooling 41 studies and 154 effect sizes, on how well self-estimates of cognitive ability match psychometrically measured ability. the correlation was 0.33.

that one number kills both sides of the usual argument. it isn't zero — people genuinely do pick up signal about where they sit, which is the reel's point. and it isn't close to one — self-ratings are a moderate correlate at best, which means anyone treating their own confidence as proof is running on a fairly noisy instrument. you know something. you don't know as much as the feeling suggests.

the part i was partly wrong about

there's a real, replicated version of "smart people don't know it" — it's just much narrower than the meme.

everyone cites Dunning-Kruger for the low end: bottom performers wildly overestimate where they rank. that's the famous half. the other half is in the same work and almost nobody mentions it. in Kruger and Dunning's own follow-up, they describe top performers as estimating their raw scores pretty accurately while slightly but reliably underestimating their percentile rank among peers.

the explanation they give is the false consensus effect — high performers assume the task was about as easy for everyone else as it was for them, so they overrate the room. read that carefully, because it's precise. they know what they can do. they're wrong about the comparison. so if you're capable and you keep being surprised that other people can't do the thing, that's not modesty and it's not impostor syndrome. it's a calibration error about everyone else, and it's fixable.

the other real version: the impostor pattern

worth naming properly so you can tell it apart from ordinary uncertainty.

Clance and Imes described the impostor phenomenon in 1978 from clinical work with high-achieving women: people with objective evidence of success who internally experience themselves as intellectual phonies and attribute what they've done to luck, timing or having fooled someone. it's real, it's documented, and it is emphatically not the same claim as "intelligent people generally don't know they're intelligent."

the difference matters. the impostor pattern is a specific internal experience that persists against evidence. the reel's target was the much lazier social rule — the idea that anyone who states their own ability out loud has thereby disqualified themselves. those are different things and the second one is a manners rule pretending to be a fact.

socrates never said it

the single most-quoted line in this whole argument isn't a quote.

"i know that i know nothing" appears nowhere in Plato in that form. the passage people mean is Apology 21d, which in Jowett's translation runs closer to "i neither know nor think that i know" — and in Cary's, "what i do not know i do not think i know either." those are not the same sentence. one is a claim of total ignorance. the other is a claim about not overstating your confidence, which is precisely what calibration means.

and prominent Plato scholars have argued the popular version shouldn't be attributed to Plato's Socrates at all. so if someone drops it on you to end a conversation about intelligence, it's worth knowing the line has been doing work its author didn't assign it.

the calibration log

stop arguing about whether you're smart. measure whether you're accurate.

this is the asset in this guide. confidence is easy to fake to yourself and impossible to fake against a scoreboard. you make dated predictions with a stated confidence, you seal them, and four weeks later you score them. the output is a single number that tells you whether you're over-confident, under-confident, or calibrated — which is the only version of "how smart am i" that has a right answer.

THE CALIBRATION LOG

step 1 — write 10 predictions. mix of work, money, people, your own
behaviour. each must be resolvable on a fixed date and must be
falsifiable. beside each, a confidence: 50 / 60 / 70 / 80 / 90 / 95%.
never write 100.

  DATE  |  PREDICTION                              | CONF | RESULT
  ------|------------------------------------------|------|-------
  04/10 | the client signs before the 18th         | 70%  |
  04/10 | i ship the landing page this week        | 90%  |
  04/10 | [x] replies within 48 hours              | 60%  |
  ...   | ...                                      | ...  |

step 2 — do not look at it for four weeks.

step 3 — score. group by confidence band:
  of everything you called 70%, what fraction came true?
  of everything you called 90%, what fraction came true?

  band hit rate  ~=  stated confidence   -> calibrated
  band hit rate  <   stated confidence   -> over-confident
  band hit rate  >   stated confidence   -> under-confident
                                            (this is the common one
                                             for capable people)

step 4 — one sentence per miss: what did i not know that i could
have known? not "i was unlucky". that answer is banned.

do four rounds before you trust the result. ten predictions is
too few to mean anything on its own.

say it without being insufferable

the goal is accuracy out loud, not volume.

here's the thing the reel didn't have time for. the reason "smart people don't say they're smart" became a rule is that the people who do say it are usually saying it as a global claim — "i'm smarter than most people" — which is unfalsifiable, unhelpful and immediately makes everyone want you to be wrong. a domain claim does the same job and nobody can object to it, because it's checkable.

THE DOMAIN MAP — fill this in before you talk about any of it

  DOMAIN            | EVIDENCE (a thing that happened)  | RANK I'D CLAIM
  ------------------|-----------------------------------|----------------
  e.g. pricing      | 3 rate raises, none lost a client | top of my room
  e.g. writing      | 40k people read the thing         | above average
  e.g. spreadsheets | i google INDEX MATCH every time   | below average

rule: you don't get a row without an evidence cell. a claim with an
empty evidence cell is a feeling, and feelings go in the log, not
the map. you must have at least one below-average row or you have
not done the exercise.

then swap the global claims for domain claims out loud. left column is what people actually say. right column does the same job and survives contact with a room.

PHRASE SWAPS

"i'm smarter than most people here"   -> "pricing is the thing i'm best at.
                                          ask me that one."
"honestly this is easy for me"        -> "this one's quick for me. what's
                                          slow for you? let's trade."
"i don't know why nobody sees this"   -> "i might be wrong. here's what i'd
                                          have to be wrong about."
"i'm not that smart"  (when you are)  -> "i'm good at X. i'm bad at Y."
"i know that i know nothing"          -> "i'm about 70% on this."

good to know

  • 0.33 is a moderate correlation, not a strong one. that means your self-read is real signal and a bad instrument at the same time. anyone who quotes it as "people know how smart they are" is overselling it, and anyone who quotes it as "people have no idea" is underselling it.
  • the reel used Tesla and Oppenheimer as rhetoric. i'm deliberately not putting quotes in their mouths here — i couldn't verify any, and a made-up quote from a dead physicist is exactly the move this guide is arguing against. the argument works fine without them.
  • IQ is one narrow measure and the whole conversation gets dumber when people treat it as a single score for a person. the calibration log is more useful than any test result you could buy, because it measures the thing that actually costs you money when it's off.
  • under-confidence has a specific price and it isn't humility. it's the email you didn't send and the number you didn't say. run the log for a quarter and if every band comes back higher than you stated, you now have written evidence that you've been sandbagging yourself, and that's harder to argue with than a pep talk.
  • being right about your own ability and being pleasant to be around are not in conflict. state the domain, show the evidence, keep at least one honest below-average row, and get on with it. that's Off-Script Rich.

proof

  • Freund & Kasten (2012), Psychological Bulletin — meta-analysis of 41 studies, 154 effect sizes, r = 0.33 between self-estimated and measured cognitive ability
  • Kruger & Dunning (2002) reply, University of Michigan — top performers estimated raw scores accurately but underestimated their percentile rank, attributed to the false consensus effect
  • Clance & Imes (1978), APA PsycNet record — the original description of the impostor phenomenon in high-achieving women
  • "I know that I know nothing" — the phrase appears nowhere in Plato in that form; Apology 21d translations and the scholarly dispute over attribution
