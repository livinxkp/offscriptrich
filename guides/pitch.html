the emails that got the yes

hey, it's KP. you commented PITCH, so here it is. the reel gave you the idea. this is the actual text — four emails, fill in the blanks, send them in this order. the reply that opens negotiation, the counter, the follow-up when they go quiet, and the one that walks away.

who this is for

you, if you're sending pitches and getting nothing back, or if a brand has replied and you're about to freeze on the price. it's built for UGC and single-deal sponsorships. skip it if you're negotiating a year-long ambassador contract or anything with equity in it — that needs a lawyer, not a template.

why this matters

i got $2,000 for two videos. Collabstr's 2026 report, which looked at more than 21,000 collaborations, puts the average UGC payout at $154 and says 80% of all collaborations come in under $300, with only 2% above $1,000. i don't have a huge audience. the gap wasn't reach.

the gap was that i sold rights and amplification instead of selling a video, and i said one sentence at the right moment. both of those live in an email. the follower count you can't change this month. the email you can rewrite in ten minutes.

what actually changed in the pitch

i stopped asking and started handing them something already built.

early on i was sending the pitch everyone sends: here's me, here's my numbers, would you like to work together. nothing. the version that got answers did three things differently. it led with a specific idea for them, not with my stats. it used my actual situation. i'm building this whole challenge on camera, so the collab had a story attached instead of being one more sponsored slot. and it asked for a short call or a yes on a concept, never for a budget.

the structural rule underneath all of it: the first email sells the idea, the second email sells the price. if you put a number in email one, you've priced the smallest possible version of the deal before you know what they actually want.

SUBJECT LINES THAT GET OPENED
  idea for [BRAND] — [SPECIFIC THING, 4 words]
  [PRODUCT] + [YOUR ANGLE] — quick concept
  re: [THING THEY JUST LAUNCHED]

NEVER:
  "collaboration opportunity"
  "partnership inquiry"
  "love your brand!"

THE 4-LINE PITCH BODY
  1  the specific idea, one sentence, no preamble
  2  why it's you and not anyone else (your actual situation)
  3  one proof point — a number, not an adjective
  4  one ask: "want me to send the full concept?"

keep it under 120 words. no media kit attached on email one.

email 1 — the reply that opens negotiation

the whole job of this email is to get their scope without giving your price.

a brand emails you. the instinct is to answer the question they asked, which is usually "what are your rates?" don't. a rate without a scope is a guess, and it's always a guess low. you need four things before a number exists: deliverables, where it runs, how long they hold the rights, and whether they're putting ad spend behind it. ask for all four in one breath so it reads as process, not stalling.

Subject: re: [their subject]

hi [NAME],

thanks for reaching out — [PRODUCT] is genuinely something i
[use / have been looking at], so this is an easy yes in principle.

before i send a number, four quick things so i can price it
properly rather than guess:

1. deliverables — how many pieces, which formats, and are they
   posting on my channels, yours, or both?
2. usage — where does the content run, and for how long?
3. paid — are you putting spend behind it, and is that boosting
   your own post or running ads from my handle?
4. exclusivity — is there a category exclusivity period?

send those over and i'll come back with a full quote and a
timeline within 24 hours.

[YOUR NAME]

this email does something else quietly. it tells them you know what whitelisting is. brands price differently for creators who know the vocabulary, because they can't sell you a rights grab as a compliment.

email 2 — the counter

this is the one that turned one video into two and a small number into $2,000.

they came back wanting one video. the move is not to argue about the price of one video. the move is to attach the price to what they're actually asking for, which in my case was full perpetuity rights and paid amplification, and then offer the better-value version at your real number. you are not discounting. you are repricing the deal they wrote.

Influee benchmarks usage rights at 30–50% of base rate and whitelisting at roughly 30% of base per month. InfluencerFee puts rights and whitelisting together at +80–150% of the base creative fee. perpetuity is the extreme end of that — they keep it forever, so there is no version where it's a rounding error on one video.

Subject: re: [their subject]

hi [NAME],

happy to make this work. one note on scope before i quote.

what you've described isn't one video — it's one video plus
perpetual usage and paid amplification. those are the parts
that carry the value, because the asset keeps working for you
long after the post does.

my rate for a single video at that rights level is $[HIGH].
for two videos at the same rights level it's $[TARGET], which
gives you a second creative to test against and works out
meaningfully better per asset.

if perpetuity isn't essential, i can do [12 months] usage at
$[LOWER] for the two and you can renew later at [RATE].

happy either way — which shape works for your budget?

[YOUR NAME]

three things are load-bearing in there. the word "test" — performance brands buy variants. the fallback at shorter usage, which gives them a way to say yes that isn't "less money for the same thing". and ending on a question so the ball is genuinely with them.

email 3 — the follow-up when they go quiet

most deals don't die, they stall. quiet is a scheduling problem far more often than a rejection.

your contact has a manager, a quarter, and eleven other creators. follow up on a fixed cadence and stop reading silence as an answer. the follow-up that works adds something new. a date, a slot, a piece of content. that way it isn't just you asking again in a smaller voice.

Subject: re: [their subject]   (keep the same thread, always)

hi [NAME],

bumping this in case it got buried.

i've got a production slot open [WEEK], and after that my next
availability is [LATER DATE]. if [BRAND] wants this live before
[THEIR LAUNCH / SEASON], [WEEK] is the one to take.

no pressure if the timing's wrong — just let me know either way
and i'll plan around it.

[YOUR NAME]

---
CADENCE — from your last email, then stop
  day 3    bump, same thread
  day 8    bump + new information (a slot, a result, an idea)
  day 17   the walk-away email below
  day 90   fresh pitch, new angle, clean slate

email 4 — the one that walks away

the walk-away is not a threat and it is not sulking. it closes the loop and it converts more often than the third bump.

you send this when the number is under your floor, or when they've gone silent past day 17. it works because it removes the decision pressure entirely, which is frequently the exact thing that was blocking a yes. write it so that if they never reply you're still fine, because sometimes they won't, and that's a real outcome too.

Subject: re: [their subject]

hi [NAME],

i'll take this off your plate.

$[THEIR OFFER] sits below what i can do for [deliverables +
perpetuity + paid usage] — at that rights level my floor is
$[YOUR FLOOR], so i don't think i'm the right fit for this
budget and i'd rather say that than deliver something thin.

if the scope changes — shorter usage window, no paid
amplification, or a single deliverable — the number moves and
i'd genuinely like to work on this.

either way, good luck with [CAMPAIGN]. i'll keep an eye out.

[YOUR NAME]

note what it never does: it doesn't drop the price on the way out, and it doesn't apologise. it names a floor and leaves a specific door open. lowering your number in the walk-away email is the single fastest way to teach a brand that your rate card is decorative.

before you hit send, and what has to be in the contract

the email gets you the yes. this list is what stops the yes turning into a problem.

two things to get right. first, payment: Campaign reported in September 2025 that up to 87% of creators have been paid late or had payment issues, citing payment platforms Tipalti and Lumanu, with net-30, net-60 and net-90 terms stacking into months of waiting. get half up front and you've removed most of that risk before it exists.

second, disclosure. the FTC's Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255, require you to disclose a material connection to the brand — which the FTC defines to include the brand "paying you or giving you free or discounted products or services." the guides also say the disclosure has to sit with the endorsement itself, not on a profile page or behind a "more" link. put it in the contract so nobody asks you to bury it later. do that once and the income you build is income you actually keep. that's Off-Script Rich.

PRE-SEND CHECKLIST
  [ ] deliverables, usage window, paid amplification and
      exclusivity are all written down in numbers
  [ ] your walk-away floor is decided BEFORE you send
  [ ] your quote is padded 15-25% — they will counter
  [ ] no media kit, no life story, under 200 words
  [ ] one question at the end

MUST BE IN THE CONTRACT
  [ ] 50% on signature, 50% within 14 days of delivery
  [ ] usage window with a START and an END date
  [ ] paid amplification window dated separately
  [ ] exclusivity category named narrowly, or struck out
  [ ] revisions: 2 included, $[RATE] per round after
  [ ] kill fee if they cancel after you've shot
  [ ] disclosure per FTC 16 CFR Part 255, placed in-caption
      and in-video, and the brand can't ask you to remove it

good to know

  • the counter email only works if you can actually deliver two videos. don't upsell into a scope you can't shoot in their timeline — one late delivery costs you the repeat deal, which is worth more than the upsell.
  • perpetuity is the term to fight hardest on, not the price. a brand running your face in ads three years from now is a deal you sold once and they use forever. a 12-month window with a renewal rate is almost always the better trade.
  • most inbound is a gifting offer dressed as a partnership. the FTC counts free product as a material connection, so a product-only deal hands you the full disclosure obligation with no fee attached. it's fine to want the product — just know you're being paid in product, and say so in your reply.
  • send the walk-away. genuinely. i've had brands come back within 48 hours at the number after that email, and the ones that don't were never going to pay it.
