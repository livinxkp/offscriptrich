money BANKS

the account setup uneven income needs

hey, it's KP. you commented BANKS, so here it is. the reel gave you the names. this is the plumbing — which account money lands in, which one you are not allowed to touch, how to work out your own tax hold-back instead of copying mine, and the four things to switch on tonight.

who this is for

you, if your income arrives in lumps — one brand deal, three quiet weeks, then two payouts the same afternoon. built for self-employed money: sponsorships, UGC, affiliate, platform payouts, invoices. skip it if you're salaried on the same date every month. you don't need four accounts, you need one and a standing transfer.

why this matters

one account hides everything. money lands, the balance looks healthy, you spend against it, then the quarterly tax payment and the annual renewals turn up in the same fortnight. that isn't a discipline problem — a single balance physically cannot tell you which of those dollars were ever yours.

and the deadlines aren't optional. the IRS expects self-employed income paid in instalments across the year, not once in April, and you're in that system if you expect to owe $1,000 or more after withholding and credits. four accounts is the simplest machine that makes the money you owe unavailable to spend before the date arrives.

how the money actually moves

one account takes money in, three take money out, and you touch it once a week.

the four jobs: LANDING receives every dollar and spends none. OWED holds the money that was never yours — tax, and anything you've been paid for but not yet delivered. FIXED pays every recurring bill. FREE is the only balance you consult when deciding anything.

money moves one direction only: owed first, fixed second, free last, nothing backwards. the discipline isn't the split — it's that owed is a one-way door. and here's what most versions of this get wrong: only one of your four numbers is a percentage. fixed is a dollar figure you already know, free is a remainder. one decision to get right.

RULES
  landing keeps $0 target balance after transfer day
  owed is one-way: money goes in, only the IRS
    and your state take it out
  fixed gets a dollar figure, not a percentage
  free is a remainder, never a target
  if free runs thin you raise the top line or cut
    the fixed list. you do not borrow from owed.

account one — landing

every dollar of income arrives in exactly one place, and nothing is ever bought from it.

a business checking account if you're set up as an entity, a second personal checking account if you're not yet. the rule that makes it work is physical: no card from this account in your wallet. not discipline, friction.

one inbound account means your year reconciles from one statement instead of six, and whoever does your books charges by the hour. it also ends trying to remember in March whether a purchase was for work. mixing brand money with grocery money costs you a weekend.

account two — owed

this is the one that turns the tax bill from an event into a date on a calendar.

the dates are fixed. the periods run January–March, April–May, June–August and September–December, due April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year. for 2026: April 15, June 15, September 15 2026 and January 15 2027 — and you can skip the January one if you file the return by February 1 2027. a date landing on a weekend or holiday rolls to the next business day.

now your rate, because there is no universal one. it depends on filing status, household income, deductions, your state, and how much of your gross is net profit. but there's a floor you can't go under. self-employment tax alone is 15.3% — 12.4% social security plus 2.9% Medicare — applied to 92.35% of net earnings. that's roughly 14 cents of every net dollar before a cent of income tax, so anyone telling you 10% is describing something arithmetically impossible. get a CPA or an enrolled agent for this part. i'm not one, and an hour with one costs less than a badly estimated quarter.

WORK OUT YOUR OWN HOLD-BACK RATE

ROUTE A — you filed a return last year
  effective rate = total tax on last year's return
                   / total income on last year's return
  hold-back = that rate + 5 points
  (the margin is because your income is growing and
   last year's rate under-describes this year)

ROUTE B — first year, no return to look at
  use the Form 1040-ES worksheet. it walks you through
  expected income, the standard deduction for your
  status (2026: $16,100 single or married filing
  separately / $32,200 joint / $24,150 head of
  household), then SE tax and income tax.

FLOOR CHECK — whatever you get, it cannot be below:
  net earnings x 0.9235 x 0.153  = SE tax alone
  + federal income tax at your bracket
  + state and local, if your state has it

RECALIBRATE every quarter, not every year.
  too much held = a refund. too little = a penalty.
  only ever adjust this number DOWNWARD with a
  return or an accountant in front of you.

two things nobody switches on. the default is four equal instalments, which with uneven income means overpaying in a dead spring and scrambling in a loud autumn — the annualized installment method on Form 2210 lets you pay in proportion to when you actually earned. most software buries it three clicks deep. and the safe harbours: you generally dodge the underpayment penalty by paying 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's, whichever is smaller — 110% of last year's if your prior-year AGI was over $150,000.

account three — fixed

every recurring charge comes out of one account, against money that was already sitting there.

tally what bills you whether or not you post this month: rent, phone, insurance, editing software, cloud storage, the domain, gear on finance, the accountant. one number. move it in on the same date monthly and keep one extra month of it parked permanently as float. that float turns a brand paying 45 days late into a scheduling annoyance instead of a crisis. every bill here is on autopay, and the one card that lives here is autopaid in full from here.

account four — free

the only balance you look at when you're deciding whether you can do something.

what's left when owed and fixed are handled. travel, dinners, the lens, the thing you built this for. the system was never about restriction — it's that this number is finally true, so you can spend it without the background hum of wondering what's coming.

when free consistently runs above what you spend, the surplus leaves the cash system and goes to investing. an IRA is capped at $7,500 for 2026, or $8,600 if you're 50 or older. be clear-eyed about one thing though: invested money isn't deposit-insured. stocks, bonds, mutual funds, crypto and annuities are not FDIC insured, full stop. SIPC covers a brokerage failing to hand back what it held — $500,000 per customer, $250,000 of that for cash — and covers nothing about value going down. keep tax money in a bank, not the market.

where to open them, without chasing a rate

four accounts only works if none of them quietly charges you for existing.

know how the insurance works first, because it isn't per account. the FDIC standard is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category. three accounts in your own name at one bank share a single $250,000 — they don't get $250,000 each. the category part is what people miss: single and joint are separate categories at the same bank. for joint accounts, each co-owner's share of every joint account at that bank is added together and insured to $250,000, so two co-owners are covered to $500,000 between them. the FDIC's own example: two people holding a $350,000 CD and a $150,000 savings account at one bank are insured to $500,000.

think categories, not picks: business checking for landing, online savings for owed, ordinary checking for fixed and free, with brokerage cash accounts a fourth category worth a look. names you'll come across — Ally, Capital One 360, Marcus, Chase, Fidelity, Schwab — are examples of those categories, not recommendations. i'm not telling you where to bank. compare them yourself, and deliberately don't compare on the advertised rate: rates move constantly, and the difference on one month of expenses is a rounding error against one missed quarterly payment. that's Off-Script Rich.

good to know

  • the "six withdrawals a month" savings rule is not federal law any more. the Federal Reserve deleted the six-per-month limit on convenient transfers from savings deposits on 24 April 2020 and has no plans to re-impose it — but banks are permitted, not required, to stop enforcing it. yours may still charge. read its fee schedule, not a forum.
  • there's no penalty at all if you end up owing under $1,000 after withholding and credits — worth knowing in a thin year before you panic about a missed quarter.
  • an app is not a bank. deposit insurance attaches to an FDIC-insured bank, per bank — so if your tax money sits at a fintech, the protection runs to the bank behind it, not the app's own ledger. find that bank's name before the balance gets large.
  • the split isn't the achievement. holding the owed balance untouched for nine straight months is. the first time you raid it "just this once" the system is over, and you rebuild it in April at the worst possible moment.

proof

  • FDIC — Understanding Deposit Insurance: $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category; stocks, bonds, mutual funds, crypto and annuities not insured
  • FDIC — Your Insured Deposits: the single account rule, the joint account rule, and the $350,000 CD + $150,000 savings example insured to $500,000
  • FDIC BankFind Suite — the official lookup for whether an institution is FDIC-insured
  • IRS — When to pay estimated tax: the four payment periods and the April 15 / June 15 / Sept 15 / Jan 15 due dates, and the weekend-and-holiday roll
  • IRS Form 1040-ES (2026) — the 2026 payment dates, the February 1 2027 filing exception, the 2026 standard deduction amounts, the 92.35% multiplier and the $184,500 social security wage base
  • IRS — Estimated taxes: the $1,000 threshold, and the 90% / 100% / 110%-over-$150,000 AGI safe harbours
  • IRS Topic 306 — underpayment penalty, the four-equal-amounts default, and the annualized installment method for income received unevenly (Form 2210)
  • IRS — Self-employment tax: the 15.3% rate, 12.4% social security plus 2.9% Medicare, and the $400 net earnings filing threshold
  • IRS Direct Pay — free, no sign-in, covers estimated tax, changeable or cancellable within 2 days of a scheduled payment
  • IRS — IRA contribution limits: $7,500 for 2026, $8,600 if age 50 or older
  • SIPC — what SIPC protects: $500,000 per customer including a $250,000 cash sublimit, and no protection against a decline in value
  • Federal Reserve — Savings Deposits FAQ: the six-per-month transfer limit deleted 24 April 2020, permitted but not required to be suspended by banks, with no plans to re-impose
