buy the business, bring the ai

hey, it's KP. you commented BOOMER, so here it is. the roadmap — what these businesses actually sell for, how the financing really works after the rule change next week, the letter i'd send a seller, and what i'd automate in the first 90 days.

who this is for

this is for you if you can run people and read a P&L, and you'd rather own something with customers on day one than spend two years looking for product-market fit. skip it if you want passive — you're buying a job first and an asset second, and anyone telling you otherwise hasn't done it.

why this matters

the reel gave you the thesis. here's the size of it. McKinsey's work on the ownership transition puts more than half of all US small-business owners over 55 — up from roughly 30% in 2002 — with one in four now 65 or older. by 2035 about six million small and mid-sized businesses will face an ownership transition, against roughly 4.5 million in the prior decade. more than a million of those are genuinely viable to sell, representing up to $5 trillion in enterprise value.

and here's the part that should actually move you. of the exits that happened in 2022, 92% were closures. only 5% were sales and 3% were transfers. these businesses aren't being fought over. most of them just switch the lights off because nobody asked.

what these actually sell for

the price is a multiple of owner earnings, and it's lower than you think. BizBuySell's Q1 2026 report covers 2,345 completed sales: median sale price $350,000, median cash flow $165,256, median revenue $713,404 — a 2.7x multiple of cash flow. across all sectors the average earnings multiple sits around 2.58.

by trade, using BizBuySell's multiples of seller's discretionary earnings:

  • auto repair & service shops — 2.85x
  • HVAC — 2.83x
  • electrical & mechanical contracting — 2.77x
  • plumbing — 2.61x
  • landscaping & yard services — 2.49x
  • pest control — 2.46x
  • cleaning — 2.25x

read that the right way. a business throwing off $165k to its owner trades for around $350k–$450k. that's the whole opportunity in one line: you are buying two-and-a-half to three years of earnings, and financing most of it.

how it gets financed

the SBA 7(a) program is the standard instrument for this and the rules just changed — know which set applies to you.

  • maximum 7(a) loan: $5 million. as of 4 July 2026 you can combine 7(a) and 504 for up to $10 million total SBA-backed financing.
  • equity injection: 10% minimum on a change of ownership. under SOP 50 10 8.1, effective 1 October 2026, that 10% is fixed for an initial acquisition and cannot be reduced by the lender.
  • a seller note can cover part of your 10% — but only if it's on full standby, meaning no principal and no interest payments for the entire term of the SBA loan, and it can't be more than half of the required injection.
  • maturity: up to 10 years for an acquisition without real estate; up to 25 years when owner-occupied real estate is included.
  • personal guarantees are part of the deal. this is real recourse debt against you, not a fund's capital.

so on a $400,000 deal: roughly $40,000 of injection, of which up to half could be a standby seller note if the seller agrees. find a lender through the SBA's own Lender Match tool, and talk to three, because appetite for acquisition deals varies enormously between banks.

the letter that starts everything

most of these owners have never been approached, and 92% of exits are closures — you are frequently the only person who asked. don't lead with money. lead with the thing they actually care about, which is what happens to their crew and their name. send this by post, not email, to owners you find on the state licensing register for a trade in one metro area:

Dear [name],

I'm [your name]. I live in [city] and I'm looking to buy one
[HVAC / landscaping / cleaning] business in this area and run it
myself — not to merge it into anything, not to flip it.

I'm not an agent and I'm not sending this to hundreds of people.
I pulled your name off the [state] licensing register because
[specific reason — years in business, the reviews, the fleet].

If you've ever thought about what happens to the business when you
step back, I'd like to buy you a coffee and hear how you built it.
No obligation and nothing to sign. If the timing is wrong, tell me
and I'll leave you alone.

Three things I'd commit to up front:
  1. Your staff keep their jobs and their pay.
  2. The business keeps your name.
  3. You get paid, and I'd want you around for a handover.

[phone] — call or text me directly.

[your name]

send 50. expect a handful of calls. the first two conversations will be bad and the third will teach you what to ask.

diligence: the questions that kill deals

almost every bad small-business deal dies on one of six things, and all six are checkable before you pay for a quality-of-earnings report. run this on the first call and the first site visit:

PRE-LOI CHECKLIST — kill it fast, before you spend

REVENUE
[ ] top customer as % of revenue. over 20% = concentration risk
[ ] recurring vs one-off split — get the actual contracts
[ ] 3 years of tax returns, not the owner's spreadsheet
[ ] do revenue and the bank statements agree? check 3 months

THE OWNER
[ ] how many hours a week do they actually work?
[ ] whose phone do customers call — theirs or the office?
[ ] are they the licence holder? if so, what happens on day one?
[ ] will they sign a non-compete and stay 90 days?

THE CREW
[ ] average tenure of the top 3 people
[ ] is anyone paid under the table or as a contractor wrongly?
[ ] who is the real second-in-command, and do they know about this?

THE ASSETS
[ ] age and condition of vehicles and equipment — get the list
[ ] deferred maintenance the seller has been putting off
[ ] is the lease assignable, and how long is left?

THE SYSTEMS
[ ] what software runs scheduling, invoicing, payroll? or is it paper?
[ ] how many calls go unanswered in a week — ask for the phone log
[ ] how are they getting new customers today?

THE DEAL
[ ] will the seller carry a note, and will they put it on standby?
[ ] does the cash flow cover debt service with room? model it at
    the multiple you're paying, not the one you want

the phone-log question is the one nobody asks and it's the one that tells you where the upside is.

the first 90 days of ai

do not rebuild the business. automate the front door, then leave everything else alone for a quarter. the biggest recoverable loss in these companies is the same everywhere: the phone rings while the owner is under a sink, nobody answers, and that customer calls the next name on the list. Jobber, which builds software for exactly these trades, launched an AI receptionist for this in August 2025 and reports it handles over 20 calls a week per business on average with no human intervention. its own survey of 1,001 US consumers found 96% say response time influences their purchasing decision.

FIRST 90 DAYS — in this order, one at a time

Days 1-30  — DON'T CHANGE ANYTHING
  ride along on jobs. answer the phone yourself for two weeks.
  write down every question customers ask. that's your training data.

Days 31-60 — THE FRONT DOOR
  1. AI call answering, 24/7, that books straight into the calendar
     (Jobber's Receptionist, or Goodcall — both do after-hours and
     both push bookings into scheduling software)
  2. automated quote follow-up: every quote gets a text at day 2
     and day 7 if it hasn't been accepted
  3. review requests sent automatically the day a job closes

Days 61-90 — THE BACK OFFICE
  4. invoice chasing on a schedule instead of when you remember
  5. job notes dictated on-site and written up by AI, not at 9pm
  6. one report every Monday: jobs booked, quotes out, cash in

DO NOT touch pricing, staff or branding in the first 90 days.

that order matters. every item in days 31–60 adds revenue from demand the business is already generating and losing. the back-office items only buy back your time. do the revenue ones first.

good to know

  • an SBA loan is personally guaranteed. if the business fails you still owe the money. that's the single biggest difference between this and starting something — the downside is real and it's yours.
  • the seller-note-as-equity trick only works on full standby: no principal, no interest, for the entire SBA loan term, which is typically 10 years. plenty of sellers say yes to "carrying paper" and no to that. clarify it before you write an LOI.
  • the rules changed on 1 June 2025 and change again on 1 October 2026. a guide, a podcast or a lender quoting the older SOP is giving you stale terms — always ask which SOP version they're underwriting to.
  • the AI upside is almost never the reason a deal works. a business that only clears its debt service if your automation lands is a business you're overpaying for. underwrite it at today's numbers and treat the AI as the bonus.
  • the trades with the best multiples on paper — auto repair, HVAC — are also the ones where a licence sits with the retiring owner. if you can't hold or hire that licence, there is no deal, however good the P&L looks.
  • i'm not your lawyer, lender or accountant. everything above is sourced and linked so you can take the actual documents to people who are.
