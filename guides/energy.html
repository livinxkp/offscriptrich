the power constraint, in filings

hey, it's KP. you commented ENERGY, so here it is. this isn't a list of things to buy — i've deliberately not written one. it's the thing underneath the story: how much nuclear capacity actually exists, what the announced datacentre deals buy when you read the announcement instead of the headline, how long the grid queue really takes, and the honest record on small reactor timelines. with the dates to watch at the end.

who this is for

you, if you keep seeing "ai needs power" and want to know what the physical constraint actually is and where it's measured. skip this if you came for tickers and price targets — there are none here, on purpose.

why this matters

compute is a supply chain with a hard physical floor at the end of it. chips can be air-freighted. electrons cannot be imported, and neither can the transmission line, the transformer or the interconnection agreement. that makes power the one part of this buildout that runs on permitting and construction clocks rather than on announcements.

which is why it's worth understanding rather than trading. the numbers here come from EIA, FERC, the NRC, Berkeley Lab and the companies' own releases, almost all on a published schedule — so you can check them yourself instead of waiting to be told what they mean.

the base you're starting from

the existing nuclear fleet is smaller than the conversation implies, and it is not growing quickly.

per EIA, as of March 2026 the United States had 96 operating commercial reactors at 57 plants across 28 states, with a combined net summer generating capacity of 98,441 MW. nuclear supplied 17% of US generation in 2025. that's the entire pool any datacentre deal contracts against today — and since a gigawatt is 1,000 MW, a single 1,920 MW agreement is roughly two per cent of the national fleet.

demand is going the other way. EIA's September 2026 Short-Term Energy Outlook forecasts US electricity sales of 4,135 billion kWh in 2026 and 4,211 billion kWh in 2027, with the commercial sector alone up 3.3% then 2.7% and accounting for 63% and 56% of the total increase. EIA attributes the record levels to "data center development and increased manufacturing activity".

what the announced deals actually buy

most of the nuclear headlines are contracts over existing electrons, not new ones.

read them in order. Constellation and Microsoft, 20 September 2024: a 20-year PPA for the Crane Clean Energy Center, roughly 835 MW, restarting a stopped unit, originally "expected to be online in 2028". Constellation and Meta, 3 June 2025: 1,121 MW from Clinton for 20 years beginning June 2027, which Constellation's own release describes as supporting relicensing and continued operation after the state credit programme expires, plus a 30 MW uprate. Talen and Amazon, 11 June 2025: 1,920 MW from Susquehanna through 2042, full volume "no later than 2032". NextEra and Google, October 2025: restarting the roughly 610 MWe Duane Arnold plant, shut since 2020, on a 25-year agreement, targeting early 2029.

notice what that list mostly is: restarts, relicensings and uprates. the 30 MW uprate is genuinely new capacity. the rest is existing output contracted to a new buyer, or a plant brought back. both matter — but a contract that reallocates existing megawatts doesn't add megawatts, and that distinction is the whole game.

HOW TO READ A POWER ANNOUNCEMENT — six questions

1. NEW OR EXISTING?
   restart / relicensing / uprate / new build.
   only "uprate" and "new build" add electrons.

2. HOW MANY MW, AND WHEN AT FULL VOLUME?
   headline MW is often the end state. find the ramp.
   "full volume no later than ____"

3. WHAT'S THE TERM, AND WHEN DOES IT START?
   a 20-year deal starting in 2027 delivers nothing
   for the next 18 months.

4. WHAT APPROVALS ARE NAMED IN THE RELEASE?
   NRC? state and local permits? transmission
   reconfiguration? each one is a separate clock,
   and the release usually lists them honestly.

5. BINDING OR NOT?
   look for "letter of intent", "non-binding",
   "subject to", "up to", "intends to".

6. WHO CARRIES THE COST OF THE GRID UPGRADE?
   if the release doesn't say, that question is
   still open — and it's the one regulators are
   currently deciding.

the interconnection queue is the real clock

the binding constraint isn't generation that doesn't exist. it's generation that exists on paper and can't get connected.

Berkeley Lab counts it every year. in the 2026 edition of Queued Up, as of end-2025 there were 2,061 GW of active capacity in US interconnection queues — 1,312 GW generation plus 749 GW storage. by resource: solar 773 GW, storage 749 GW, gas 253 GW (up 86% year on year), wind 220 GW, geothermal 4.8 GW, nuclear 10.4 GW — up 96% year on year and still a rounding error.

then the two that matter most. the median project that came online in 2025 took 61 months from interconnection request to commercial operation, against 36 months in 2015 and 22 in 2008. and of everything that requested interconnection between 2000 and 2020, only about 19% of projects — 13% of capacity — reached commercial operation by the end of 2025, with roughly 75% of capacity withdrawn. a queue position is not a power plant.

one limit on that report: it counts generation and storage, not load. datacentres connect as large customers through a separate process, and that process is being rewritten now — FERC docket RM26-4-000, "Interconnection of Large Loads to the Interstate Transmission System", covering loads generally above 20 MW. FERC said on 16 April 2026 it would act by June 2026, after over 3,500 pages of comments.

small reactors: what the record actually shows

every serious timeline in this field has moved, and mostly in one direction. plan around that, not around the press release.

be specific about it. NuScale's Carbon Free Power Project — six 77 MW modules, 462 MW — published an "updated target price" of $89 per megawatt-hour on 9 January 2023, and on 8 November 2023 NuScale and UAMPS terminated it outright because "it appears unlikely that the project will have enough subscription to continue toward deployment". TerraPower's Natrium announced on 15 December 2022 that its 2028 in-service date was moving back at least two years, for lack of commercial capacity to make its HALEU fuel. and the recent large reactors are the benchmark: per EIA, Vogtle Unit 3 was scheduled for 2016 and entered commercial operation in July 2023; Unit 4 was scheduled for 2017 and entered service in May 2024 — "originally expected to cost $14 billion", actually "more than $30 billion".

hold the progress alongside it, also dated. the NRC issued NuScale's US460 standard design approval on 29 May 2025 and Kairos Power's Hermes 2 construction permits on 21 November 2024. on 4 March 2026 it approved a construction permit for TerraPower's 345 MWe Kemmerer Unit 1 — the NRC calls it "the first commercial reactor the NRC has approved for construction in nearly a decade" — and TerraPower began construction on 23 April 2026. one schedule even moved earlier: Constellation said on 23 September 2025 it had accelerated Crane's restart to 2027. licensing has sped up. construction and fuel are where it still slips.

HOW TO SCORE A DATE WHEN IT ARRIVES
  [ ] did it hold, move earlier, or slip?
  [ ] by how long, and what was the stated reason?
      fuel supply / licensing / construction /
      transmission / offtake
  [ ] is the reason specific to this project, or does
      it apply to every project using that fuel,
      that reactor design, or that grid region?

  that last question is the only one that generalises.
  a single project slipping is a project. the same
  REASON appearing across three projects is a
  constraint, and constraints are what you can
  actually reason about.

good to know

  • megawatts and megawatt-hours are not the same thing and the mix-up is everywhere. MW is capacity, the size of the tap. MWh is energy, the water that came out. a nuclear plant runs near its rating almost all year; solar and wind do not. so "1,000 MW of solar announced" and "1,000 MW of nuclear contracted" are not comparable quantities of electricity, and treating them as equal invalidates the comparison before it starts.
  • a restart is not a new plant, and the release will usually tell you. most nuclear-plus-datacentre deals signed so far contract output that already exists or recently existed. that changes who gets the power and whether the plant keeps running — both important — without adding much to national supply. read only the headline and you'll systematically overestimate how many new electrons are arriving.
  • the grid connection is a separate queue from the generation queue, and its rules aren't finished. Berkeley Lab's report explicitly excludes load interconnection, which is the datacentre side — the side FERC's open docket is about. the best-documented dataset in this area is silent on the exact thing everyone argues over.
  • every date on this page is a target, not a delivery. an approved licence, a signed 20-year contract and a started construction project are three different distances from electricity. treat any timeline you read — including the ones i've listed — as the earliest plausible date rather than the expected one.
