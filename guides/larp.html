how to larp the person you want to be

hey, it's KP. you commented LARP, so here it is. the reel said the most successful people are basically larpers. here's how to actually do it: write the character, talk to yourself like him (there's research on why that works), make the cut list, and run the daily walk script i use on the run to $100k.

who this is for

you, if you can describe the person you want to be in detail and still wake up acting like the old one. skip it if "pretend" means faking results to other people. this is acting the part in private until it's true, never claiming it's true before it is.

why pretending works at all

it's not magic, it's two well-studied effects.

stepping outside yourself helps you persist. in the "Batman effect" study, four- and six-year-olds were given a boring task with a tempting distraction nearby. the kids told to think of themselves as a character like Batman, and to ask "is Batman working hard?", stuck with the task longer than kids who thought about themselves as themselves.

talking to yourself in the third person calms you down. in adults, using your own name or "you" instead of "i" when you think through a stressful moment helped people handle social stress and perform better in experiments by Ethan Kross and colleagues. it creates a bit of distance, and from a distance you think more like an advisor than a participant.

so "what would he do?" isn't just a nice line. it's a distancing tool.

the character sheet

write the person you're playing. specific enough that you'd recognise him walking in.

CHARACTER SHEET

name i call him: _________________ (your name works)

what he's already done: _______________________________
how he starts his day: ________________________________
how he talks about money: _____________________________
what he says no to without explaining: ________________
how he walks into a room: _____________________________
what he does when it goes badly: ______________________
three people he'd spend more time with: _______________

the cut list

the reel said cut the people he wouldn't be friends with. careful version: cut time and inputs, not necessarily people.

THE CUT LIST                          cut / reduce / keep

accounts i follow that make me feel behind:   ______
group chats that are only complaining:        ______
habits he wouldn't have:                      ______
time sinks after 10pm:                        ______
people who only show up to doubt it:          ______ (less time,
                                                      not a speech)

the daily walk script

20 minutes, every day, out loud or in your head. use your own name, second person. that's the distancing part.

"[your name], you already hit ______.
you remember how it felt the day it landed.
today you're the guy who ______ before noon.
when ______ happens today, you ______.
you don't need to check the number. you know where it's going.
what's the one move today that guy would make? ______
go."

i do this on the run to $100k. i talk to myself like the guy who already hit it, and ask him what today's move is. the answer is usually obvious and slightly uncomfortable. that's the one. that's Off-Script Rich.

good to know

  • private larp, public honesty. act like him in your habits, never in your claims. telling people you've done what you haven't is just lying with extra steps.
  • the research is narrower than the reel. the Batman study was young children, and the self-talk studies were about handling stress in the moment. neither proves pretending changes your whole life overnight. they show the distancing trick helps you persist and stay calm, which is the useful bit.
  • judge it on behaviour. if after two weeks you're doing more of what he'd do, it works. if you only feel better, it's a nice walk.

proof

  • White et al. (2017), The "Batman Effect": improving perseverance in young children, Child Development · children who took a character's perspective persisted longer
  • Kross et al. (2014), Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism: how you do it matters, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology · non-first-person self-talk and social stress
