eleven laws, and how to use each one

hey, it's KP. you commented LAWS, so here it is. the reel ran through eleven in under a minute. here's the one-page version: each law stated properly, where it comes from, one way to use it this week, and a cheat sheet you can screenshot.

who this is for

you, if you like having a name for things you've noticed, and want a short list of ideas that explain how work, people and decisions actually go. skip it if you want the academic version. each one here links to the original if you want to go deeper.

the eleven

1. parkinson's law. work expands to fill the time available for its completion.

use it: give tasks deadlines shorter than feels comfortable. a two-hour job given a week takes a week.
C. Northcote Parkinson, in an essay in The Economist, 1955.

2. hofstadter's law. it always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account hofstadter's law.

use it: whatever estimate you give, add a buffer, then don't trust the buffer either.
Douglas Hofstadter, Gödel, Escher, Bach, 1979.

3. hanlon's razor. never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by carelessness.

use it: the late reply, the missed invite, the blunt email. assume busy before you assume hostile.
credited to Robert J. Hanlon, 1980, from an older idea.

4. the pareto principle. roughly 80% of the effects come from 20% of the causes.

use it: find the 20% of clients, habits or posts that drive most of your results, and do more of those.
named after Vilfredo Pareto's land-ownership observations, popularised by Joseph Juran. the 80/20 split is a rule of thumb, not a constant.

5. the peter principle. in a hierarchy, people tend to get promoted until they reach a job they're not good at.

use it: being great at a job is not evidence you'll be great at managing it. ask what the next role actually needs.
Laurence J. Peter, 1969. a 2019 study of sales promotions found real evidence for it.

6. hick's law. the more options you give someone, the longer it takes them to decide.

use it: fewer choices on your menu, your offer, your outfit rotation. one clear option beats five okay ones.
W. E. Hick, 1952, and Ray Hyman, 1953.

7. goodhart's law. when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

use it: chase views and you get views, not fans. check the number still means what it meant when you picked it.
Charles Goodhart, 1975. the popular phrasing is Marilyn Strathern's, 1997.

8. the dunning-kruger effect. people with low skill in an area tend to overestimate it, partly because the skill to judge competence is the same skill they lack.

use it: when you feel most certain about something new to you, that's the moment to ask someone better.
Justin Kruger and David Dunning, 1999. it's about specific skills, not general intelligence, and its size is still debated.

9. occam's razor. among explanations that fit the facts equally well, prefer the one that needs the fewest assumptions.

use it: the post flopped because the hook was weak, not because the algorithm is punishing you personally.
after William of Ockham, 14th century. it's a tie-breaker, not proof.

10. chesterton's fence. don't remove a fence until you know why it was put up.

use it: before you scrap a rule, a habit or a process, find out what problem it was solving.
G. K. Chesterton, The Thing, 1929.

11. brooks's law. adding people to a late software project makes it later.

use it: new people need onboarding and add communication lines. extra hands help early, not at the deadline.
Fred Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month, 1975.

the three i use most: parkinson for my week, hick for anything i'm selling, goodhart every time i look at my views. that's Off-Script Rich.

good to know

  • these are heuristics, not physics. "laws" is a nickname. each one describes a strong tendency with plenty of exceptions.
  • the pop versions drift. Dunning-Kruger isn't "stupid people think they're smart", and 80/20 isn't always 80/20. the line under each law above has the more careful version.
  • use one at a time. pick the one that matches your current problem. eleven at once is just a list.
