score the deal before you answer

hey, it's KP. you commented DEAL, so here it is. the reel was one offer and a gut call. this is the scorecard i actually run — six criteria, a hundred points, a hard cut-off, and five things that kill a deal no matter how big the number is.

who this is for

you, if an offer has landed that looks good and you can't tell whether it is good. the scorecard works for any one-off sponsorship or UGC deal where a contract is involved. skip it if the offer is product-only with no fee — that's not a deal to score, it's a decision about whether you want the product.

why this matters

a big number is the easiest thing in the world to say yes to and the hardest thing to un-say. the terms that cost you are never in the headline — they're in the exclusivity clause, the usage window and the payment schedule, and all three are written to be skimmed past.

the specific trap is that a good offer and a good deal are different objects. an offer above your normal rate can still be the worst thing you sign this year if it locks your entire category for six months while a competitor is emailing you weekly. you can't feel that difference. you have to score it.

your floor comes first

you cannot score an offer without a number to score it against.

the floor is not your dream rate and it's not your average. it's the number below which the deal costs you more than it pays — in time, in audience goodwill, and in what you could have done with those two days instead. work it out once, write it down, and stop renegotiating it with yourself at 11pm.

and calibrate against the real market, not the screenshots. Collabstr's 2026 report, covering more than 21,000 collaborations, found 80% of all collaborations came in under $300 and only 2% cleared $1,000. that cuts both ways: it means a four-figure offer is genuinely rare, and it means you should be very sure before you turn one down.

rights, exclusivity and ad spend are priced ON TOP of this.
the floor is the bottom of the base rate. it is not the price.

the six things that decide it

every real brand deal turns on these, in this order of damage.

  • rate against your floor. not against what you hoped. the multiple of your floor is the only honest way to read a number, because a $17,000 offer and a $700 offer can be the same deal at different scales.
  • exclusivity — length and breadth. the most expensive clause in creator contracts and the one most often handed over free. "home office tech for six months" is not a detail, it's a business decision.
  • usage rights. how long they keep it and whether it runs in paid media. Influee benchmarks usage rights at 30–50% of base rate, so if rights are in the ask and not in the price, you're already under.
  • audience fit. would you post this unpaid. if the answer is no, the fee has to cover the trust you're spending, and usually it doesn't.
  • payment terms. money you get in four months is worth less than money you get in two weeks, and sometimes it's worth nothing.
  • creative control and disclosure. who has final cut, how many revision rounds, and whether they'll let you disclose it properly.

the scorecard

copy this, score honestly, and let the number decide instead of your mood.

the weights aren't arbitrary. rate and exclusivity carry the most points because they're the two that determine what the next six months look like. creative control carries the fewest because it's the easiest to negotiate after the fact. the auto-fails sit outside the scoring entirely — they end the deal at any score.

exclusivity is the line item that eats the deal

stop pricing it as a multiplier. price it as the deals you're agreeing not to do.

InfluencerFee benchmarks exclusivity at a 1.5–2x multiplier on the fee, and that's a reasonable starting point if you've got nothing else. but a multiplier has no idea what your inbound actually looks like. if you're getting one offer a month in that category, six months of exclusivity is six deals. if you're getting none, it's free money and you should take it and say nothing.

so count, don't guess. the number you need is real inbound in that specific category over the last three months.

EXCLUSIVITY FEE — the lost-deal method

  A  inbound offers in THIS category, last 3 months   ____
  B  offers per month              = A / 3            ____
  C  your close rate on those     (your own, be honest) ____
  D  your average deal in this category               $____
  E  months of exclusivity they're asking for         ____

  EXCLUSIVITY FEE = B x C x D x E                     $____

then take the HIGHER of that and (base fee x 0.5), because
exclusivity also costs you deals you'd never have counted -
the ones brands won't send once they see you're locked.

the other half of this is breadth, and it gets less attention than length. "tech" is not a category, it's your whole niche. make them name it narrowly, the specific product type and the specific competitors, and get that named list into the contract. a six-month exclusivity on three named competitors is a completely different object to a six-month exclusivity on "home office technology," and brands will write the second one and call it the first.

payment terms and the disclosure line

a rate you don't get paid isn't a rate, and a deal you can't disclose isn't a deal.

Campaign reported in September 2025 that up to 87% of creators have been paid late or experienced payment issues, citing payment platforms Tipalti and Lumanu, with net-30, net-60 and net-90 structures stacking into months. that's why payment terms carry real points on the scorecard rather than being a footnote. 50% on signature is the single highest-leverage thing you can ask for, and most brands will agree to it if you ask once, plainly, without apologising.

then disclosure. the FTC's Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255, revised in 2023, require you to disclose a "material connection", which the FTC defines as "a personal, family, or employment relationship or a financial relationship – such as the brand paying you or giving you free or discounted products or services." it has to sit with the endorsement itself, not on a profile page or behind a "more" link, and in video it needs to be at least visual if the endorsement is visual. the FTC also says plainly: "you can't talk about your experience with a product you haven't tried." that's why an untried product is an auto-fail here rather than a scoring penalty — it isn't a preference, it's the line.

THE COUNTER, not the rejection:
  - keep $17,000 for the two videos
  - exclusivity priced separately, OR
  - cut it to 90 days, OR
  - narrow it to 3 named competitors
  - product valued at retail is not part of the fee

and that's the whole point of scoring it. $17,000 is a genuinely strong number — it clears the 2% of deals above $1,000 several times over. it still fails, because an unpriced six-month category lock can cost more than the deal pays. the scorecard doesn't tell you to walk. it tells you exactly which line to counter on, which is a far more useful answer. build the thing that pays you twice instead of the thing that pays you once and closes the door. that's Off-Script Rich.

good to know

  • product value is not fee. a brand adding $2,000 of retail-priced product has added something that costs them a fraction of that and that you can't pay rent with. score it at zero and negotiate it as a bonus, never as part of the number.
  • "in-house, not an agency" is worth real points and nobody talks about it. you're talking to the person with the budget, revisions are faster, and the relationship survives the campaign. agencies churn contacts and your deal dies with them.
  • score before you reply, not after. once you've said "that sounds great" you've priced the deal emotionally and every later objection reads as you backing out.
  • the scorecard will occasionally tell you to decline something you want. that's the whole feature. if you only run it on deals you were already going to refuse, you've built a rubber stamp.
