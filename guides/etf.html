the boring method, with numbers

hey, it's KP. you commented ETF, so here it is. the reel gave you the story. this is the part i couldn't fit: what S&P's own scorecard says happens to people who try to beat the market, what the SEC says a fee actually costs you, and the four pages you fill in so you don't have to decide anything at 9am on a red day.

who this is for

you, if you've got money going in and no written rule for where it goes, or if you keep opening the app to check. skip this if you're already automated and haven't logged in since march — you're done, go outside.

why this matters

most people think investing is a knowledge problem. it isn't. the information is free and it's been the same for forty years. it's a behaviour problem wearing a knowledge costume — the tab-checking, the switching, the "just this once" position that ends up 40% of the account.

the boring method wins because it removes every moment where you could get it wrong. that's the whole mechanism. below is what that costs you to ignore, in percentages published by the people who own the index itself.

what "most retail investors avoid" actually means

the professionals who do this full-time, with teams and terminals, mostly lose to the plain index.

S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes a scorecard twice a year comparing every active US fund against the index it says it's trying to beat. it's called SPIVA. it is the least flattering document in finance and it is published by the index provider, not by a blog. here's the year-end 2025 edition:

and the obvious comeback — "fine, so i'll pick the ones that won" — has its own scorecard. of the large-cap funds sitting in the top quarter in 2023, 29% were still in the top quarter two years later. stretch it to five years and only 4.5% of the above-median 2021 funds stayed above median the whole way, against 6.25% you'd expect from a coin. they did worse than random.

the fee is the only thing you control

you cannot choose your return. you can choose your cost, and cost is certain.

the SEC ran this exact example in its own investor bulletin. $100,000, 4% a year, 20 years. at a 0.25% annual fee you end with about $208,000. at 1.00% you end with about $179,000. same market, same years, same you — about $29,000 gone. that's roughly 14% of the ending balance for three quarters of one percent a year.

and that's the polite version. the gap between a broad fund at 0.03% and a product at 1.00% is wider than the gap the SEC used. run it on your own number before you buy anything:

THE FEE DRAG CHECK

1. find the NET expense ratio on the fund provider's own page.
   not a comparison site. the provider's page. they have to publish it.

2. write it as a decimal:   0.03%  ->  0.0003
                            1.00%  ->  0.0100

3. multiply by what you expect to have invested in ten years.
   (example numbers, put yours in)
   0.0003 x $250,000  =  $75 a year
   0.0100 x $250,000  =  $2,500 a year

4. do step 3 again for the thing you were about to buy instead.

5. the gap is your annual subscription to the story.
   decide if the story is worth it. sometimes it is. usually it isn't.

one honest caveat the SEC makes itself: not every index fund is low-fee. the word "index" on the label guarantees you nothing. check the number, every time.

core and satellite, written down

you're allowed to have opinions. you're not allowed to have them with the core.

what i told the woman on the flight wasn't a ticker. it was a shape. the core is broad and whole-market and never interesting. whatever's left is your play money, and it's capped in writing before you have a good idea, because good ideas are exactly when the cap stops working. write this once, on one page, and sign it while nothing is happening:

MY INVESTING RULES — written before i need them

  what:              broad, whole-market funds
  % of total:        ____%
  bought:            every ______, automatically, on the ____th

  % of total:        ____%   <- if this is over 10% it isn't satellite
  max in one name:   ____%
  i sell when:       ______________________

the last line is the one that does the work. a plan you can't state is a feeling, and feelings get sold at the bottom.

make it automatic or it won't happen

the goal is a portfolio that keeps growing on the months you forget it exists.

my friend didn't out-think anyone. he set up transfers and then had a decade of his life. forty minutes, once:

THE SETUP

[ ] payday +1: automatic transfer, bank -> brokerage
[ ] recurring buy on the brokerage, same date every month
[ ] fractional shares ON, so the whole amount goes to work
[ ] dividend reinvestment ON
[ ] price alerts OFF
[ ] app notifications OFF
[ ] portfolio app off the phone's home screen
[ ] ONE annual calendar reminder: rebalance, re-read the rules
    page above, change nothing else. that's the only day
    you're allowed to look.

and one thing to decide today rather than in a drawdown: the automatic buy does not get paused. a falling market is the automatic buy doing the single useful thing it was built for. pausing it converts a temporary number into a permanent one.

the ten-year part nobody posts about

the timeline is the strategy. everything else is decoration.

my friend was in credit card debt ten years ago. paid it off, kept buying, crossed a million in his early forties. there is no clip in that. no entry, no exit, no call. the whole thing is one decision repeated 120 times while the internet told him he was missing something.

i'm not going to hand you a return assumption, because nobody honest will. run the equation yourself at three different rates and look at the spread — that spread is your plan, not the middle number:

HOW LONG — the only equation you need

FV = P x [ ((1 + r)^n - 1) / r ]

FV = what you end with
P  = what you put in per year
r  = the annual return you're assuming, as a decimal
n  = years

run it three times: a pessimistic r, a middle r, an optimistic r.
paste the line into any spreadsheet:

  =FV(rate, years, -annual_contribution, 0, 1)

the honest read is the low one. if the low one still gets you
somewhere you'd accept, you have a plan. if it only works on
the optimistic number, you have a bet.

the unpopular part isn't the fund. it's agreeing to be unremarkable for a decade while people with worse outcomes have better stories. that's Off-Script Rich.

good to know

  • SPIVA is a survivorship-adjusted scorecard, and that's the point. funds that closed mid-period are counted, not quietly deleted. that's why the numbers look worse than the ones a fund's own marketing shows you — the marketing is showing you the survivors.
  • the fee isn't the whole cost. expense ratio is the published line. what you actually lose also includes the spread you pay to trade and the tax you trigger by trading. every one of those goes up when you touch it more. the cost of being interesting is not a single number.
  • the short-run numbers wobble and people over-read them. active funds lost to the S&P 500 at a 79% rate over full-year 2025 and 67% over the first half of 2026. that's a real improvement and it means nothing for a ten-year plan — the twenty-year figure is 92.89% and it has never been kind.
  • "boring" has a failure mode too. a core that's 100% one country's large companies is boring and concentrated at the same time. broad means broad. if you can't say what your core excludes, you haven't checked it — that's what the PICKS guide is for.
