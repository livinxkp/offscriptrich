four steps, in the order that works

hey, it's KP. you commented STEPS, so here it is. the reel gave you the four steps. what it didn't have room for is the part that actually decides whether this works: why they go in that order. the order is the strategy. the steps are obvious. below is each one, the reason it sits where it sits, and a worksheet where you fill in your own numbers instead of mine.

who this is for

you, if you've read ten money posts and still don't know what to do first. that's the only question that matters when everything feels urgent at once. skip this if you already have a written sequence you're executing; you don't need a fifth opinion, you need another month of doing it.

why this matters

the order isn't a preference. it's the difference between progress and a loop. the Fed's household survey found 55% of adults had money set aside for three months of expenses in 2025 — unchanged from 2024 and down from a 2021 high of 59%. in the same survey, 63% said they'd cover a surprise $400 expense entirely with cash, savings or a card paid off at the next statement, which means over a third wouldn't. and the CFPB's own numbers show what happens to the ones who don't: 40% of consumers with no emergency savings had debt 60 or more days past due, against 5% of those in the highest savings group. 79% had trouble paying a bill in the past year, against 6%.

read that as mechanism, not morality. with no cash buffer, every other financial move you make gets reversed by the first surprise. that's why step one is step one, and why people who skip it spend years feeling like they're doing everything right and going nowhere.

step one — the buffer

build a cash floor in a separate insured account before you optimise anything else.

this goes first because it's the only step that protects the other three. no buffer means the next car repair goes on a card at 20-something percent, and you've just converted a $600 problem into a two-year one. the number isn't sacred. the CFPB deliberately won't name one, saying it "depends on your situation" and that even a small amount provides some security. so pick yours off your own spending, not a round number off the internet.

where it sits matters: FDIC insurance covers $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category, so safety is handled. the rate isn't. the FDIC's published national rate for savings accounts was 0.37% in September 2026 — that's the number a high-yield account has to beat by a wide margin before it's worth the transfer. check the current one, don't take mine.

step two — the match

if your employer matches retirement contributions, take the full match before you do anything clever.

this sits second, ahead of paying off debt, for one reason: the match is the only thing on this list with a deadline. you cannot go back and claim last year's match. debt will still be there next month and your ability to attack it isn't capped by the calendar. a match is also a one-shot percentage on the dollar the moment it lands, which is a bigger single-year number than almost any interest rate you're carrying.

two mechanics people get wrong. first, your own contributions are always 100% vested, and the IRS is explicit about that. but the employer's match can be on a vesting schedule, and the maximums allowed are a three-year cliff or six-year graded (0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, 100%). if you're leaving in eight months, read your plan document before you count that money. second, the match eats into the same ceiling you do: for 2026 the total of your deferrals, the employer match and any nonelective contributions can't exceed $72,000 (or $80,000 counting catch-up, up to $83,250 if you're 60 to 63). your own deferral limit for 2026 is $24,500, with an $8,000 catch-up at 50+ and $11,250 for ages 60 to 63.

step three — the expensive debt, and the one place the maths loses

highest interest rate first costs you less in total interest. smallest balance first is more likely to get finished. pick on purpose, not by mood.

this is the step where everyone repeats a slogan and nobody reads the research, so here it is. paying highest-rate-first is arithmetically optimal — there's no argument there. a 2023 study in the Southern Economic Journal put a price on doing it the other way: households paying smallest-balance-first paid an extra 1.8% to 4.3% in interest, which the author totals at $46.2bn to $53.9bn transferred from borrowers to lenders nationally, and found the penalty lands hardest on lower-income and Black households because of how their debts are structured.

but. Gal and McShane, in the Journal of Marketing Research in 2012, looked at real debt-settlement accounts and found that closing individual accounts predicted finishing the programme — and the dollar size of the accounts closed wasn't statistically significant. in their simulation, people consistently paying off smallest balances were about 14 percentage points more likely to complete after one year than people paying randomly. Kettle and colleagues, in the Journal of Consumer Research in 2016, found the same lever: concentrating repayment on one account raises motivation, strongest when it's the smallest one.

so the honest version: a 4% penalty on a plan you finish beats a perfect plan you quit in month five. that's the trade. now decide it in writing.

step four — the automatic percentage

a fixed share of every paycheck leaves before you see it, into tax-advantaged space first.

this is last, and it isn't because it matters least. it's the step that compounds. it's last because the first three remove every reason you'd have to interrupt it. a buffer means you don't sell to cover a repair. the match is already running. cleared high-rate debt means nothing is quietly out-earning your portfolio in the wrong direction. now automation actually stays automatic, which is the whole mechanism.

the 2026 numbers worth knowing: the IRA limit is $7,500, with a $1,100 catch-up at 50+. Roth IRA eligibility phases out between $153,000 and $168,000 for single filers and $242,000 and $252,000 married filing jointly. and the one nobody mentions — the Saver's Credit gives you a credit for contributing at all if your income is under $40,250 single, $60,375 head of household or $80,500 married filing jointly. that's the government paying you to do step four. for which funds go in the account, that's PICKS.

none of these four steps are impressive. that's the feature. there's no version of this where the order is exciting and it still works — the thing that gets you out is doing the unglamorous one first while everyone argues about the last one. that's Off-Script Rich.

good to know

  • the match-before-debt call is genuinely contested, and you should know why. a match is a one-time gain on the dollar; a high APR compounds against you every year you carry it. if you're sitting on a very high rate and a small match, the sequence flips and reasonable people will tell you so. the deadline argument is what tips it for me — you can't retroactively claim a match, and the vesting schedule means you should read your plan document before you treat it as banked.
  • "three to six months" is a convention, not a finding. the CFPB deliberately refuses to name a number and tells you to size it off your own past surprises. what the data actually supports is a threshold effect: consumers with at least one month of income saved scored 61 on the CFPB's financial well-being measure against 40 for those with none. the first month buys you most of the peace.
  • the smallest-balance method has a real, measurable price and it's still sometimes the right answer. 1.8% to 4.3% extra interest is not a rounding error and anyone who tells you the order doesn't matter is wrong. anyone who tells you the arithmetic is the only input is also wrong. the completion research is just as real. the failure mode is switching methods every few months, which costs you both.
  • every limit on this page expires. contribution limits, phase-out ranges, Saver's Credit thresholds and the FDIC national rate all move, usually annually, sometimes monthly. these are the 2026 figures and the September 2026 rate. the mechanism holds; the numbers don't. links are below so you can check them yourself next january.

proof

  • Federal Reserve — Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025: Savings and Investments — 55% of adults with three months of expenses set aside, unchanged from 2024, down from 59% in 2021; 63% able to cover a $400 expense with cash, savings or a card paid at next statement
  • CFPB — Emergency Savings and Financial Security (PDF) — 40% vs 5% for debt 60+ days past due, 79% vs 6% for trouble paying bills, and the financial well-being scores of 61 vs 40 at the one-month-of-income threshold
  • CFPB — An essential guide to building an emergency fund — the amount "depends on your situation", and that even a small amount provides some security
  • FDIC — Deposit insurance — $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category
  • FDIC via FRED — National Rate: Savings (SNDR) — 0.37% for September 2026
  • IRS — 401(k) limit increases to $24,500 for 2026, IRA limit increases to $7,500 — the $24,500 deferral limit, $8,000 and $11,250 catch-ups, $7,500 IRA limit and $1,100 IRA catch-up, Roth phase-out ranges, and Saver's Credit income limits
  • IRS — 401(k) and profit-sharing plan contribution limits — the $72,000 overall 2026 limit on annual additions, $80,000 with catch-up and $83,250 for ages 60 to 63, and that employer matching counts toward it
  • IRS — Retirement topics: vesting — employee elective deferrals are always 100% vested; three-year cliff and six-year graded schedules for employer contributions
  • Gal & McShane (2012), Journal of Marketing Research 49(4), 487–501 — "Can Small Victories Help Win the War? Evidence from Consumer Debt Management" — account closure, not dollar size, predicted programme completion; ~14 percentage points at one year
  • Kettle, Trudel, Blanchard & Häubl (2016), Journal of Consumer Research 43(3), 460–477 — "Repayment Concentration and Consumer Motivation to Get Out of Debt" — concentrating repayment on one account raises motivation, strongest on the smallest balance
  • Hamilton (2023), Southern Economic Journal 89(3), 830–859 — "Two steps forward, one step back? Quantifying the pecuniary costs of debt account aversion and the debt snowball" — the 1.8%–4.3% extra interest, the $46.2bn–$53.9bn aggregate, and the distributional findings
