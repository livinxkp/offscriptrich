how to check it yourself, monthly

hey, it's KP. you commented SLOWDOWN, so here it is. the reel gave you the headlines. this is what sits underneath them: five things people actually point at when they say "bubble", what each one measures, the exact filing or factsheet it lives in, and the strongest argument on both sides. i'm not calling a top or a bottom. i'm handing you the instruments.

who this is for

you, if you hold an index fund or a few ai names and you're tired of forming a view from other people's captions. skip this if you want a verdict — there isn't one in here. there's a dashboard and where each dial is mounted.

why this matters

a bubble argument and a bubble are different objects. the argument is a story about the future and nobody gets graded on it. the indicators are a time series — they had a value last quarter, they have a different value now, and you can read both without anyone's permission.

every one of these numbers moves every ninety days, which is why this page is a routine rather than a conclusion. ten minutes a month reading four filings beats ten hours a month reading takes about four filings.

indicator one: capex against the cash coming in

one ratio tells you whether the buildout is paid for by the business or by the balance sheet.

both halves sit on the same statement, so this one is hard to argue with. three months to 30 June 2026: Microsoft's operations produced $55.4bn and it spent $35.8bn on additions to property and equipment. Alphabet's operations produced $39.1bn and it spent $44.9bn — capex ran ahead of operating cash flow — then raised $49.6bn of debt in June. Meta: $31.9bn in, $30.1bn out, $784m of free cash flow. Amazon's trailing-twelve-month operating cash flow was $161.4bn, up 33%, with trailing free cash flow an outflow of $7.6bn on a $66.1bn rise in property and equipment purchases.

bear read: spending has crossed the line where the business funds it. bull read: these are four of the most cash-generative companies that have ever existed, borrowing at investment grade against contracted demand.

CAPEX COVERAGE — do this per company, per quarter

coverage = operating cash flow / capital expenditure

where to get both numbers:
  the quarterly earnings press release, cash flow statement.
  line 1: "net cash provided by operating activities"
  line 2: "purchases of property and equipment"
           (Microsoft calls it "additions to property
            and equipment"; Meta adds "principal payments
            on finance leases" — include it)

reading it:
  above 2.0   comfortable, funded out of the business
  1.0 - 2.0   funded, but the cushion is thinning
  below 1.0   the gap is being funded by debt or cash pile

then check, same filing: did long-term debt go up?
if coverage falls below 1.0 AND debt is rising AND capex
guidance is still rising, that is the combination worth
writing down. one of the three alone is not news.

indicator two: how much of your index is one trade

you can own a "diversified" fund and still have a third of it in one theme.

concentration isn't a feeling, it's a published line on a fund factsheet. on the SPDR S&P 500 ETF factsheet dated 30 June 2026, the fund held 504 names and the top ten were 36.31% of it. NVIDIA alone was 7.50%, Apple 6.57%, Microsoft 4.29%, Amazon 3.61%, the two Alphabet share classes 3.24% and 2.58%, Broadcom 2.77%, Micron 2.01%, Meta 1.91%, Tesla 1.83%. re-read it, don't remember it — it moves with every price.

bear read: a drawdown in one theme now moves the whole index, so "i'm diversified" does less work than it used to. bull read: cap weighting is supposed to do this, the weights followed the earnings, and an index that refuses to hold the winners has its own failure mode.

THE CONCENTRATION CHECK — five minutes, once a month

1. open your fund's own page. the provider's, not a
   comparison site. find "top ten holdings" and the
   "as of" date. write the date down; a stale factsheet
   is the most common mistake here.

2. write down:
     top 10 combined  ____ %
     largest holding  ____ %      name: __________
     second largest   ____ %      name: __________

3. do it again for every equity fund you own, then add
   up how much of your TOTAL portfolio sits in the single
   largest name across all of them. most people have it
   two or three times without knowing.

4. compare to last month's line. the number you care
   about is the change, not the level.

5. decide the rule BEFORE it matters:
   "if one name is over ____% of everything i own,
    i will ______________________."

indicator three: how long they say a chip lasts

the useful life a company assigns its servers changes its reported profit without changing a thing it spent.

it's in the accounting policy note and it's worth finding. Meta's 10-K puts servers and network assets at five to five and a half years. Amazon's 10-K depreciates servers and networking equipment over five to six years. Alphabet's says servers and network equipment run up to six years. the mechanism: a longer assumed life spreads the same spend over more years, so annual depreciation is lower and reported profit is higher. shorten it and profit falls with no change in cash.

you can watch the charge build. Meta discloses depreciation on servers and network assets of $7.32bn in 2023, $11.34bn in 2024 and $13.36bn in 2025, and total depreciation and amortisation of $12.36bn for the six months to 30 June 2026 against $8.24bn a year earlier. bear read: if accelerators go obsolete faster than the booked life, current earnings are borrowed from later ones. bull read: they control the workloads, older accelerators get pushed down to inference, and the charge is plainly ramping rather than hidden.

THE USEFUL-LIFE CHECK — annual, in the 10-K

1. open the 10-K on sec.gov. search the document for
   "useful li" — it catches both "life" and "lives".

2. write down the range given for servers / network
   equipment, per company. it is stated in years.

3. next year, check the same line. what you are looking
   for is a CHANGE. an extension raises reported profit;
   a shortening lowers it. either way the company has to
   say so, and either way the cash it spent is unchanged.

4. then find, in the same filing, the depreciation charge
   and compare its growth rate to revenue growth.

   depreciation growing much faster than revenue
   = the buildout is now hitting the income statement.
   that is expected, not scandalous. the question is
   whether revenue follows it.

indicator four: who is paying whom

when the supplier helps fund the customer who buys from the supplier, one deal can look like demand twice.

the announcements say it plainly, which is why you read the announcements. NVIDIA and OpenAI announced a letter of intent on 22 September 2025 for at least 10 gigawatts of NVIDIA systems, with NVIDIA intending to invest up to $100bn "progressively as each gigawatt is deployed", first phase targeted for the second half of 2026. in an 8-K filed in October 2025, AMD disclosed a warrant for 160 million shares at $0.01 each, vesting in tranches from the first gigawatt of MI450 deliveries to full vesting at six gigawatts purchased, with price targets escalating to $600. and NVIDIA's 10-Q for the quarter ended 26 July 2026 shows $51.2bn of non-marketable securities and five direct customers at 22%, 14%, 13%, 11% and 10% of accounts receivable.

bear read: vendor financing and warrants can turn one pool of capital into several reported relationships. bull read: staged equity and milestone warrants are ordinary in a capital-intensive ramp, and a letter of intent is not a delivered order.

indicator five: the spend and the booked revenue

most of the argument is about a timing gap, not a fraud.

put the two lines side by side for the same quarter. Alphabet spent $44.9bn on property and equipment in the three months to 30 June 2026; Google Cloud's revenue that quarter was $24.8bn. Amazon reported AWS net sales of $42.2bn, up 37%. Microsoft reported Microsoft Cloud revenue of $59.3bn for the quarter and $214.4bn for the fiscal year, with Azure and other cloud services up 43%.

bear read: cash goes out now, the cloud line includes plenty that isn't ai, and nobody publishes an "ai revenue" number you could check. bull read is also a number: Microsoft's commercial remaining performance obligation was $678bn at its 2026 year end — contracted revenue not yet recognised. one quarter of capex against one quarter of revenue is the wrong comparison when the asset lasts five years and the contract runs longer. that cuts both ways, and that's the honest state of it.

the monthly dashboard

same list, same order, same day each month. the point is the change, not the level.

print this or paste it into your notes app. everything on it is free and public, and none of it requires you to have an opinion before you start. do it for a year and you'll have something almost nobody arguing about this online has: your own series. that's Off-Script Rich.

AI BUILDOUT DASHBOARD — first weekend after earnings season

1. CAPEX COVERAGE                     check: quarterly
   where: each company's earnings press release,
          cash flow statement (investor relations page,
          or the 8-K exhibit 99.1 on sec.gov)
   record: operating cash flow, capex, coverage ratio
   concerning: coverage under 1.0 AND rising debt AND
               raised capex guidance, two quarters running

2. CAPEX GUIDANCE vs LAST QUARTER     check: quarterly
   where: same release, outlook section; Meta states a
          full-year range in its release
   record: the range, and whether it moved
   concerning: guidance raised in the same quarter that
               segment revenue growth decelerates

3. INDEX CONCENTRATION                check: monthly
   where: your fund provider's own factsheet or holdings
          page; note the "as of" date
   record: top-10 weight, largest single weight
   concerning: your true single-name exposure across all
               funds passing the limit you wrote down

4. USEFUL LIVES                       check: annually
   where: the 10-K on sec.gov, search "useful li"
   record: years assigned to servers / network equipment
   concerning: a life EXTENDED in a year when growth is
               slowing — that flatters profit for free

5. DEPRECIATION vs REVENUE GROWTH     check: quarterly
   where: income statement and cash flow statement
   record: both growth rates
   concerning: depreciation growth well above revenue
               growth for several quarters together

6. VENDOR / CIRCULAR FINANCING        check: as filed
   where: 8-K filings and company newsrooms. read the
          words "letter of intent", "non-binding",
          "warrant", "subject to", "up to"
   record: is it an order, an intention, or an option?
   concerning: the same gigawatts or dollars appearing
               in two companies' announcements

7. CUSTOMER CONCENTRATION             check: quarterly
   where: supplier 10-Q, revenue and receivables
          concentration note
   record: number of customers over 10%, and their share
   concerning: fewer customers carrying more of it

8. CONTRACTED BACKLOG                 check: quarterly
   where: remaining performance obligation, in the
          10-Q/10-K or the metrics page
   record: the balance and its growth
   concerning: backlog growth stalling while capex
               guidance keeps rising — this is the
               single most useful line on the list,
               and it is the bulls' best one too

RULE: if a number surprises you, find it in the filing
before you act on it. a screenshot is not a source.

good to know

  • the dashboard cannot tell you when. every one of these indicators has been elevated while prices kept going up, and each of them can stay elevated for years. they tell you what you are exposed to, not what happens next. anyone using them to date a crash is doing something the data does not support.
  • "ai revenue" is not a reported line. none of these companies publishes one, so every ai-revenue figure you see online is somebody's estimate wearing a filing's clothes. the closest checkable proxies are segment revenue, growth rates and remaining performance obligation — and each of them includes a lot that isn't ai.
  • fiscal calendars will trip you up. Microsoft's fiscal year ends 30 June, Amazon's and Alphabet's and Meta's end 31 December, and NVIDIA's quarters end in late January, April, July and October. comparing "latest quarter" across them without checking the period end is the most common way people accidentally compare different months.
  • the headline events and the indicators are not the same story. essays, ipo timing and executive interviews move prices and move nothing on this list. if the only thing that changed this month was who said what, then nothing on your dashboard changed, and that is a real finding rather than a boring one.
