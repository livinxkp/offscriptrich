five things to check before you buy

hey, it's KP. you commented PICKS, so here it is. the reel gave you five tickers. a ticker is the least useful thing i could hand you. it goes stale, and it tells you nothing about whether the fund is any good. so this is the thing underneath it: the five numbers i check on every index fund before it gets any of my money, where each number actually lives, and what a bad answer looks like.

who this is for

you, if you've got a shortlist of funds and no way to tell them apart except vibes and a YouTube thumbnail. skip this if you're still deciding whether to invest at all. go read STEPS first, because fund selection is the smallest decision on that list.

why this matters

here's the uncomfortable bit about list videos, mine included: two funds with almost identical names can hold wildly different things, and "index" on the label guarantees you nothing. the SEC says this out loud in its own bulletin. not all index funds have lower costs than active ones, and a fund that only samples its index may not track it.

so the skill isn't collecting tickers. it's being able to open a fund page and know within four minutes whether it does what it claims, at a cost you'd accept. that's a skill you keep. the tickers you'll replace.

one. the expense ratio, from the provider only

the only cost number that counts is the net expense ratio on the page of the company that runs the fund.

not a comparison site, not a screenshot, not me. the provider has to publish it and they publish it for free. as of 30 june 2026, Vanguard's own factsheets put VOO at 0.03%, VTI at 0.03%, BND at 0.03% and VXUS at 0.05%. those four are examples of four different jobs — US large companies, the whole US market, US investment-grade bonds, everything outside the US — and the point is the number, not the letters. the further a fund gets from "hold everything, change nothing", the higher that number climbs, and a higher number needs a reason you can say out loud.

WHERE THE REAL NUMBER IS

1. search: "<ticker> <provider name>"  ->  click the provider's domain
2. find the line labelled NET expense ratio (not gross)
3. write it down with today's date next to it
4. if the page won't tell you in one screen, that's information too

BAD ANSWERS
  - the only number you can find is on a blog
  - gross is published and net isn't
  - there's a fee waiver with an expiry date attached

two. tracking difference — did it actually deliver the index

a fund that tracks its index badly is an active fund you're paying index prices for.

every factsheet shows the fund's return next to its benchmark's return. the gap between those two lines is the whole story. a well-run full-replication fund should land just under its index by roughly its fee, and nothing more. here's what that looks like in practice, straight off Vanguard's factsheets, 30 june 2026:

FUND RETURN vs INDEX RETURN (annualised, as of 30 Jun 2026)

VOO  vs  S&P 500 Index
   1 year    22.28%  vs  22.32%    gap  0.04pp
   5 years   13.36%  vs  13.41%    gap  0.05pp
  10 years   15.47%  vs  15.51%    gap  0.04pp
   fee 0.03%  ->  the gap IS the fee. this is what good looks like.

VXUS vs  FTSE Global All Cap ex US Index
   1 year    27.42%  vs  26.74%
   5 years    8.81%  vs   8.67%
  10 years    9.97%  vs   9.97%
   ahead on the short windows, level at ten years. fine —
   but notice the wobble. sampled international indexes
   drift both ways. one good year is not skill.

THE TEST
  10-year gap roughly equal to the fee   ->  it works
  gap several times the fee              ->  ask why
  no 10-year row at all                  ->  the fund is too
                                             new to have a record

the reason VOO's gap is so clean is that it holds the index in full: 506 holdings against a 500-name index. VXUS holds 8,755 stocks against an index of 8,579. when a fund holds far fewer names than its index, it's sampling, and sampling is where drift comes from.

three. size and liquidity, using the numbers the SEC forces them to publish

you don't have to guess whether an ETF trades cleanly. there's a rule that makes the fund tell you.

this is the part almost nobody mentions. under SEC Rule 6c-11, an ETF has to post on its own website, prominently: its full daily holdings, its NAV, its market price, its premium or discount as of the previous business day, a table and line graph of premiums and discounts for the most recent calendar year and quarters, and its median bid-ask spread over the last rolling 30 calendar days. and if the premium or discount runs over 2% for more than seven trading days in a row, the fund has to say so and explain why, and leave that notice up for a year.

that is a free liquidity audit sitting on the page. use it.

THE LIQUIDITY CHECK (all of this is on the fund's own site)

[ ] net assets — write the figure and the as-of date
      for scale: as of 30 Jun 2026 the ETF share classes
      of VOO / VTI / BND / VXUS held roughly
      $979bn / $663bn / $160bn / $157bn
[ ] 30-day median bid-ask spread — compare it to the
      expense ratio. a spread wider than a year of fees
      means trading it costs more than owning it.
[ ] premium/discount graph — is it a flat line near zero,
      or does it swing?
[ ] any 2%-for-seven-days notice posted? read the reason.
[ ] how long has it existed? no 10-year row = no record.

SIZE IS NOT SAFETY. it's just a lower chance of the fund
being shut and liquidated on you mid-plan. small and clean
beats large and expensive.

four. overlap — what you already own twice

most beginner portfolios aren't underdiversified. they're the same fund bought three times.

this is the check that would have saved me the most money. compare the top-ten concentration of anything you're about to add against what's already in the account. as of 30 june 2026, VOO's top ten holdings were 37.9% of the fund, led by NVIDIA at 7.5%, Apple at 6.6% and Alphabet at 5.8%. VTI's top ten were 33.4%, with NVIDIA at 6.4%, Apple at 5.9% and Alphabet at 5.2%. VTI holds 3,531 stocks to VOO's 506 and still has nearly the same companies at the top, because both weight by size. owning both isn't diversification. it's a rounding error with extra steps.

this is also how you check the further-down-the-list ones. a tech-heavy fund, a semiconductor fund, a momentum-screened fund — QQQM, SMH and SPMO are the examples from the reel — all pull from the same pool of large US companies. run each one through this before it goes anywhere near a core position.

THE OVERLAP CHECK — four minutes, no tools

1. Rule 6c-11 means every ETF posts its full holdings daily.
   download the holdings file for the fund you own and the
   fund you're considering.
2. list the top 10 of each, with weights.
3. count how many names appear on both lists.
   0-2 of 10   ->  genuinely different jobs
   3-6 of 10   ->  partial overlap, size it down
   7-10 of 10  ->  you are buying the same thing twice
4. add the weights of the shared names in each fund.
   that's your real concentration, not the fund count.
5. write one sentence: "this fund is in my portfolio to do
   ______, which nothing else i own does."
   can't finish the sentence -> don't buy it.

five. tax and distributions — the account matters more than the fund

the same fund is a different investment depending on which account you hold it in.

funds pay out. in a taxable account those payouts are events. the IRS treats capital gain distributions from a fund as long-term gains regardless of how long you held the shares, reported to you on a 1099-DIV, and qualified dividends get taxed at the long-term rates rather than your income rate. ETFs are generally more tax-efficient than mutual funds here, and the reason is structural, not clever. they can swap securities in kind instead of selling them for cash, so fewer gains get forced out to shareholders.

the one that catches people is international. a fund holding non-US stocks pays foreign withholding tax, and the IRS lets you take a credit or an itemised deduction for foreign tax where you're subject to US tax on the same income. in a retirement account there's no US tax on that income to credit it against. same fund, different account, different outcome.

THE DISTRIBUTION CHECK

[ ] how often does it distribute? (monthly / quarterly)
      bond funds usually monthly. plan around it or ignore it,
      but know it.
[ ] has it made capital gain distributions in past years?
      the provider publishes distribution history. look.
[ ] does it hold non-US stocks? -> foreign tax is in play
[ ] which of my accounts is this going in, and why that one?

THE WHOLE THING ON ONE PAGE — run this on every fund
  1. net expense ratio, from the provider ..... ____%
  2. 10-yr fund return vs index return ........ ____pp gap
     (gap should be about equal to line 1)
  3. net assets ______  |  30-day median spread ______
     premium/discount graph:  flat / swings
  4. shared names in top 10 with what i own ... ___ of 10
     combined weight of shared names .......... ____%
  5. distribution frequency ______  foreign tax? Y / N
     account it goes in ______  because ______

  one line fails -> you don't own it yet, you're still deciding.

nobody's going to clap for a spreadsheet. but this is the difference between owning a portfolio and owning a collection of screenshots, and only one of those is still there in ten years. that's Off-Script Rich.

good to know

  • a five-fund list is usually four funds and a repeat. the honest version of most beginner portfolios is one broad equity position, one non-US position, one bond position, and a small amount you're allowed to have opinions with. if your fifth fund can't answer the "what does this do that nothing else does" sentence, it's there for the feeling.
  • the expense ratio isn't the full cost and never was. the published ratio is one line. you also pay the bid-ask spread every time you trade, and in a taxable account you pay tax on whatever the fund distributes. all three go up when you fiddle. that's why the 30-day median spread is worth as much attention as the fee.
  • "ahead of its index" is not a compliment for an index fund. VXUS beat its benchmark on the one- and five-year windows and landed exactly level at ten years. an index fund running ahead means it's sampling differently from the index, which cuts both ways. you want the boring gap equal to the fee, in both directions, for a decade.
  • every number on this page has an as-of date, including mine. expense ratios get cut, funds merge, indexes get reconstituted, providers change benchmarks. these were Vanguard's published figures as of 30 june 2026. check them on the provider's page before you act on them — that's the entire point of the guide.

proof

  • Vanguard — VOO factsheet, 30 June 2026 (PDF) — 0.03% expense ratio, 506 holdings, ~$979bn ETF net assets, top ten at 37.9%, and the fund-vs-S&P-500 return table
  • Vanguard — VTI factsheet, 30 June 2026 (PDF) — 0.03%, 3,531 holdings, ~$663bn ETF net assets, top ten at 33.4%, CRSP US Total Market Index
  • Vanguard — VXUS factsheet, 30 June 2026 (PDF) — 0.05%, 8,755 holdings against an 8,579-name index, ~$157bn ETF net assets, and the fund-vs-FTSE-Global-All-Cap-ex-US return table
  • Vanguard — BND factsheet, 30 June 2026 (PDF) — 0.03%, 11,476 bonds, ~$160bn ETF net assets, Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Float Adjusted Index
  • SEC Division of Investment Management — Rule 6c-11 website posting requirements — daily holdings, NAV, market price, premium/discount, historic premium/discount table and graph, 30-day rolling median bid-ask spread, and the 2%-for-seven-days disclosure
  • SEC / Investor.gov — Investor Bulletin: Index Funds — "not all index funds have lower costs than actively managed funds", and sampling as a cause of tracking divergence
  • SEC / Investor.gov — Characteristics of Mutual Funds and ETFs — in-kind exchanges and why ETFs generally distribute fewer taxable gains; premium and discount to NAV
  • IRS Topic no. 404 — Dividends — capital gain distributions from funds are always reported as long-term capital gains on Form 1099-DIV; qualified dividends are taxed at capital gain rates
  • IRS Topic no. 409 — Capital gains and losses — the 0%, 15% and 20% long-term rates that qualified dividends and capital gain distributions fall under
  • IRS — Foreign tax credit — credit or itemised deduction for foreign taxes where you're subject to US tax on the same income
